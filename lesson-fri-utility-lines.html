⚡ Electrical Hazard Zones

ANSI Z133 and WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation Part 19 divide the space around energized overhead conductors into zones. Understanding these zones is not optional — entering the wrong zone without the right controls can be fatal.

  • Beyond 6.1 m from conductors at voltages up to 750 V: no special electrical controls required

⚠ Critical Distinction — Deenergized vs. Deenergized AND Grounded

A line that is "deenergized" (power switched off at the substation or switch) is NOT safe to approach without maintained distances.

Reasons a "switched-off" line remains dangerous: it can be re-energized accidentally by a remote switch or auto-recloser; it may carry induced voltage from adjacent energized conductors; stored charge may remain.

ONLY a deenergized AND grounded line, confirmed in writing by a qualified electrical worker, is safe to work adjacent to without maintaining approach distances. Until you have that written confirmation, treat every line as energized.

WorkSafeBC Requirement — OHS Regulation s. 19.3–19.5

Before working within 3 metres of any overhead electrical line, the employer MUST:

  • Contact the utility to determine the line voltage
  • Obtain the required minimum approach distances for that voltage
  • Follow the utility's written instructions before work begins

This obligation applies to all workers and equipment — not only those performing electrical work.

📚ISA Exam — Electrical Hazards

ANSI Z133 Table 1 defines minimum approach distances by voltage class. The ISA exam may ask you to identify a distance from a specific voltage or to define the difference between Zone 1 and Zone 3. Know that Zone 1 is the absolute exclusion zone and that a "deenergized" line is NOT safe until grounded and confirmed by a qualified electrical worker. Safe Work Practices is 28% of the ISA exam.

🇨🇦 BC-Specific Requirements

WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation Part 19 is BC law. ANSI Z133 is an industry standard. Where the two differ, apply the more conservative (larger) distance. For Okanagan arborists, FortisBC is the primary distribution utility — know how to contact them.

ANSI Z133 — Section 5 (Electrical Hazards)

Line-Clearance Arborist Definition
Who Can Work in Zone 1?

ANSI Z133 states: "No employee shall perform tree work within the minimum approach distance of energized conductors unless the employee is trained and authorized as a qualified line-clearance arborist."

A qualified line-clearance arborist has additional electrical safety training beyond standard ISA certification. The ISA Certified Arborist credential alone does not qualify a worker for line-clearance work.

ANSI Z133 Table 1 — Key Distances
Distribution Voltages (Okanagan Residential)
  • 300 V – 4 kV: 1.5 m minimum approach distance
  • 4 – 35 kV: 3.0 m (common for Okanagan residential distribution)
  • 35 – 46 kV: 3.0 m (approaching transmission range)
  • >46 kV (transmission): Higher distances — check ANSI Z133 Table 1
BC vs. ANSI — Which Distance Applies?

WorkSafeBC distances are generally more conservative than ANSI Z133 in some voltage ranges. BC law (WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation Part 19) takes precedence in British Columbia. Always apply the more restrictive (larger distance) of the two standards. When in doubt, use the WorkSafeBC distance.

📚ISA Exam — Regulations

The ISA exam is written to ANSI Z133. Know Section 5 and Table 1. The exam may distinguish between a "qualified arborist" and a "qualified line-clearance arborist" — these are different credentials with different scopes. The exam may also ask which standard governs when two standards conflict — the answer is always the more restrictive.

🚛 Equipment Near Lines

Minimum approach distances apply to equipment just as they apply to workers. The boom of a bucket truck, the bed of a chipper, the cable of a rigging system — all must stay outside the approach zone. Equipment contacts with power lines cause the majority of electrical fatalities in the arboricultural industry.

Vehicles & Ground Equipment
Chippers, Trucks, Cranes
  • Same minimum approach distances apply to all equipment as to workers
  • A chip truck parked under a line, a crane swinging a load, a stump grinder in the root zone of a line-adjacent tree — all must maintain clearance
  • Equipment operator is responsible for knowing line location before moving
  • Pre-work site walk: identify all overhead lines before any equipment is positioned
Aerial Devices — Bucket Trucks
The Boom, Not Just the Basket
  • Must be operated by qualified personnel when approaching lines
  • The boom must maintain clearance — not just the basket/platform
  • The entire arc of boom travel must be assessed before the lift begins
  • Secondary insulation on the boom provides some protection but does not eliminate the approach distance requirement
  • Insulated aerials (IA bucket trucks) rated for line-clearance work have different distances — only when used by qualified line-clearance arborists
Cutting Equipment
Non-Conductive Tools in Zone 3
  • When working in Zone 3 (3.0–6.1 m), use non-conductive pruners, loppers, and pole saws
  • Fibreglass-handled tools preferred over metal
  • Chainsaws: metal guide bars and chains can conduct electricity — do not direct cuts toward conductors
  • Never use a metal pole saw in Zone 3 — a 6-metre metal pole saw handle effectively reaches into Zone 1
Rope & Rigging
Conductivity Hazards
  • Wet synthetic rope (polyester, nylon) can conduct electricity over short distances — do not route synthetic rope toward energized conductors
  • Natural fiber rope (manila) is less conductive than synthetic when dry — but neither is safe against contact with an energized conductor
  • When in Zone 3: use dry rope only, route rigging away from conductors, ensure no failure scenario sends rope into Zone 1
  • Rigging blocks, slings, and hardware are conductive
Ladders
Metal vs. Non-Conductive
  • Metal (aluminum) ladders must maintain 3+ m from energized conductors at all times
  • Fibreglass (non-conductive) ladders are preferred when working adjacent to lines — but still must maintain approach distances (they reduce risk if accidental contact occurs, but approach distances still apply)
  • Never lean a ladder against a utility pole above the secondary conductor level without line-clearance authorization
Orange-Painted Trees (BC)
FortisBC Hazard Flag

In BC utility corridors, orange paint on a tree trunk indicates FortisBC has flagged that tree for hazard assessment or removal. This is a notification — not an authorization to proceed. Do not work on orange-flagged trees without contacting FortisBC and receiving written instructions.

What Happens When Equipment Contacts a Line

When a piece of equipment contacts an energized conductor, the equipment body becomes energized. Anyone touching the equipment while grounded creates a path for current. Anyone on the ground near the equipment may be exposed to step potential — voltage gradient in the soil from the grounding point.

Ground crew rule: If you see equipment in contact with a line, do not approach. Call 911 and FortisBC. Wait for the utility to deenergize and confirm safe. See Tab 4 for the full emergency procedure.

📚ISA Exam — Equipment Hazards

Equipment approach distances are the same as worker distances under ANSI Z133. The exam may present a scenario involving a bucket truck boom, a rope route, or a pole saw length — ask yourself: does any part of the equipment or tool reach into Zone 1 or Zone 3? Also know that wet rope can conduct electricity and that non-conductive tools are required in Zone 3, not just "recommended."

📋 Work Planning for Utility-Adjacent Jobs

Every job within 10 metres of overhead lines requires specific pre-work steps before any equipment is unloaded or any climbing gear goes on. The 5-step sequence below is the minimum standard. Steps 1–4 must be complete before starting work. Step 5 is the emergency response — have it memorized before you arrive on site.

1
Identify — What Type of Line Is It?

Before anything else, identify every overhead line on the property and any that overhang from adjacent properties. Distinguish between:

  • Distribution lines (4–35 kV): Typical wooden pole lines in residential and commercial Okanagan neighbourhoods — most common hazard for arborists. Usually 3-wire or 4-wire uninsulated conductors on crossarms.
  • Transmission lines (>35 kV, often >69 kV): Steel towers or very tall poles, multiple conductors per phase, large insulators. Significantly greater hazard, larger approach distances.
  • Secondary service drops (<600 V): The wires running from the street pole to the house — often twisted triplex. Lower voltage, but still energized and dangerous.
  • Communication lines (telephone, cable TV, fibre): Usually the lowest wire on the pole. Lower voltage but can still carry enough current to cause injury.

If you cannot identify the voltage: assume the worst and treat as high-voltage distribution.

2
Contact — Call the Utility Before Work Starts

Get written confirmation of line voltage and required clearances. Don't guess, don't estimate, don't proceed on the homeowner's word.

  • FortisBC (distribution, Okanagan): 1-866-436-7847 — request a line hazard assessment and written clearance instructions
  • BC Hydro (transmission, some distribution): 1-888-BCHYDRO (1-888-224-9376)
  • Request: line voltage, required minimum approach distance, whether a line guard or deenergization can be arranged
    • 2
  • Obtain a written job clearance or work permit — verbal confirmation is not sufficient for WorkSafeBC compliance
3
Plan — Define the Work Envelope
2

Map out where all work will occur relative to the lines. Determine:

  • Can the entire job be completed outside the 3-metre zone? If yes, proceed with standard controls and maintain the distance.
  • If any cutting, rigging, or equipment positioning requires entering Zone 3 (3.0–6.1 m): ensure controls are in place (insulated tools, qualified supervision, written SWP).
  • If the job requires work inside Zone 1 (<3 m): stop and reassign to a qualified utility arborist, or arrange line deenergization/guarding with the utility.
  • Plan the rigging: where could a piece fall? Where could a rope travel? Does any failure scenario send material or rope into Zone 1?
4
Brief — Toolbox Talk Before Every Job

All crew members must be briefed before work starts. The brief must cover:

2
  • Location of all overhead lines, confirmed voltage, and confirmed clearance distances for this job
  • The exclusion zone: physical markers (flagging tape, cones) if needed
  • Which crew members are permitted in Zone 3, and what controls are in place
  • What the plan is if equipment contact occurs (see Step 5)
  • Who is calling 911 and who is calling FortisBC if there is an incident

Document the toolbox talk: date, location, attendees, topics covered. This is your WorkSafeBC paper trail.

5
Emergency Procedure — If Equipment or a Line Contacts a Conductor

Memorize this. Post it in the truck cab.

  • Do NOT touch the equipment or vehicle if it is in contact with an energized line. Even touching a tire can be fatal if current is flowing through the vehicle to ground.
  • Call 911 immediately. Then call FortisBC (1-866-436-7847) to report the contact and request emergency deenergization.
  • If you are inside the vehicle or equipment that has contacted a line: STAY INSIDE unless there is fire risk. You are safer inside the cab than stepping out into a step-potential field. The vehicle's rubber tires provide some insulation from ground.
  • If you must exit (fire imminent): Do not step normally. Jump clear, feet together, without touching the vehicle and the ground simultaneously. Once on the ground, shuffle or hop away with feet together or in very small steps — do not stride. Step potential decreases with distance; striding creates a large voltage difference between your feet.
  • Keep all bystanders, homeowners, and crew a minimum of 10 metres from any downed or contacted energized line. Assume downed lines are energized until the utility confirms otherwise.
  • Never attempt to remove a downed line from equipment, a vehicle, or a person.
👍
The rule of thumb: If you have to ask whether you're close enough to worry about it, you're already in the zone where you should stop and contact the utility. Three metres is smaller than most people think — it's about the length of a step ladder or a short rigging pole.
📚ISA Exam — Emergency Procedures

The ISA exam may present an emergency scenario and ask what the correct action is. Key answers: stay in the cab if equipment contacts a line; shuffle/hop rather than stride if you must exit (step potential); do not touch the vehicle from outside; keep bystanders 10 m back from downed lines. These are not instinctive responses — knowing them cold before an emergency is the point of training.

Deenergized — Safe or Not Safe?

Not Safe
⚠ Deenergized Only

Power has been switched off but the line has not been grounded by a qualified electrical worker. The line:

  • Can be re-energized by remote switch or auto-recloser
  • May carry induced voltage from adjacent energized conductors
  • May retain stored charge

Maintain full approach distances. Treat as energized.

Safe to Approach
✓ Deenergized AND Grounded

Line has been deenergized, visibly grounded at both sides of the work zone by a qualified electrical worker, and written confirmation has been provided.

  • Ground clamps visible on the conductor
  • Confirmation received in writing from the utility
  • Qualified electrical worker on-site or on call for the duration

Approach distances can be reduced — per written instructions only.

3.0 – 6.1 m

> 6.1 m

📏
3.0 metres is your default number. Unless the utility has confirmed in writing that the voltage is lower and a smaller distance applies, every overhead line in a BC residential neighbourhood gets a 3-metre exclusion zone. A step ladder is 1.8–2.4 m. Three metres is shorter than most people picture.
