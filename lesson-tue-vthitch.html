VT Hitch (Valdotain Tresse)

We covered Blake's Hitch. This is its evolution. The VT Hitch is the modern standard for SRT work positioning — more versatile, better in a friction ring, and the hitch of choice for climbers transitioning to single-rope technique. Know how it's built, why it grips differently from Blake's, and when you'd choose one over the other.

⬡ What Is the VT Hitch?

The Valdotain Tresse (VT Hitch) is a braided friction hitch developed by French arborist/climbing instructor Bedrich Petzl (and popularized widely in the tree care industry). "Valdotain" refers to the Aosta Valley climbing tradition; "tresse" is French for braid or plait — which describes exactly how the hitch is constructed.

Unlike the Blake's Hitch, which wraps sequentially around the host rope, the VT Hitch interlocks its own strands in a braided configuration that grips the host rope through both friction and mechanical interlock. This produces a hitch with superior performance in a friction ring (like the Petzl Chicane or Singing Rock Ring), which is where most contemporary SRT climbing systems use it.

  • Primary application: SRT (single rope technique) work positioning with a mechanical friction ring or redirect
  • Also used for: DRT positioning when used with a ring or redirect; backup in rope wrench systems
  • Common pairing: VT Hitch + Rope Wrench + tether = standard SRT working configuration
  • Cord requirement: Requires a VT-compatible climbing hitch cord, typically 7–8 mm; a spliced eye on each end is standard (and significantly improves performance)
📌 SRT vs DRT Context

Single rope technique (SRT) uses one rope going straight to the anchor — the climber ascends and descends the same line. DRT (doubled rope technique) runs the rope through the anchor so both ends reach the ground. VT Hitch is used in both systems but is most associated with modern SRT setups. Blake's Hitch can work in DRT but is not typically used in SRT primary systems.

⬡ Anatomy of the VT Hitch

The VT Hitch uses two strands of the hitch cord that braid around the host rope, crossing at each interlock point. The braided geometry creates grip through both friction and mechanical interlock — different from Blake's sequential wraps.

Key Components

  • Spliced eyes: A proper VT Hitch uses a cord with a sewn or spliced eye at each end. While a knot-and-girth-hitch method exists, spliced eyes provide a stronger, cleaner, more consistent hitch geometry and are the professional standard
  • Two strands: The cord is girth-hitched (or the eyes are looped) around the carabiner, creating two working strands that braid around the host rope
  • Interlock points: Each time the strands cross creates an interlock that grips the host rope — typically 3–5 crossings depending on cord diameter and host rope diameter
  • Both carabiners: One carabiner at each eye — top cab connects to a redirect or ring; bottom cab connects to harness or rope wrench tether

⬡ How to Rig the VT Hitch

This describes the spliced-eye configuration, which is the standard for professional use. The hitch is rigged rather than tied — it is assembled onto the rope rather than knotted in hand first.

Girth-hitch both eyes to a carabiner at the top. This creates two equal strands hanging parallel below the carabiner, one on each side of the host rope.
Cross the strands: Take the left strand and pass it behind/around the host rope to the right side. Take the right strand and pass it around to the left side. The strands have now swapped sides — this is the first cross-over.
Repeat the crossing 2–4 more times (total 3–5 crossings), working downward. Each crossing wraps both strands around the host rope, increasing the bite of the hitch.
Bring the bottom eyes together and clip both onto the bottom carabiner. The carabiner is your connection point to the harness (and/or rope wrench tether).
Dress and test: Even out the crossings so they sit parallel and uniform on the host rope. Load gently first — the hitch should grip immediately. Advance by cupping the hitch body and pushing upward.
📋 Field Note — Practice

The VT Hitch looks simple but requires precise geometry to perform correctly. Crossings that are uneven, twisted, or mis-counted significantly change grip behaviour. Spend time rigging this at ground level with a static rope until the motion is automatic and the hitch always looks the same. Many climbers use a 5-crossing configuration as the default; some drop to 3 on certain rope/cord diameter combinations. Test your specific rope and cord combination before climbing.

⬡ How It Grips — vs. Blake's Hitch

Understanding how the VT Hitch grips explains why it works so well with friction rings and why it behaves differently from Blake's under load.

  • Blake's grip: Sequential wraps tighten around the host rope through friction alone — a single mode of grip. It works directly on the rope surface.
  • VT grip: The braided crossings create a camming and wedging action — the hitch tightens through both friction AND the mechanical interlock between strands. Under load, the strands pull against each other through the host rope, increasing grip.
  • In a friction ring: The VT Hitch is designed to be used in a friction ring (like a Petzl Chicane, Singing Rock Ring, or similar). The ring acts as a redirect that changes the hitch's loading geometry — allowing descent control while keeping the hitch functional. Blake's Hitch does not perform well through a friction ring.
  • Rope compatibility: VT Hitch has better cross-diameter tolerance than Blake's — it can function acceptably even when cord and host rope diameters are closer together, because the mechanical interlock adds grip beyond just friction.
  • Temperature sensitivity: Both hitches are friction-dependent. In cold or icy conditions, test before committing weight. VT Hitch performs marginally better in wet conditions due to the mechanical component.

⬡ When NOT to Use the VT Hitch

⚠ Limitations
  • Without a friction ring in SRT: The VT Hitch alone on a single rope is not optimally configured for SRT descent without a ring or redirect to change the geometry. Pair it with a ring or use a rope wrench system.
  • Knotted-end cord (no spliced eyes): Without proper spliced eyes, hitch geometry is compromised — knots at the eyes create bulk that distorts the braid. This is not a strict prohibition but noticeably degrades performance.
  • On ropes with significant surface damage or glazing: Any friction hitch's grip is compromised on worn rope. Inspect host rope condition before climbing.
  • As a sole life safety connection: ANSI Z133 requires two connections. VT Hitch + work positioning does not satisfy both — a secondary lanyard or backup is required at all times.
  • Substituting for a mechanical progress capture in SRT ascent: The VT Hitch is primarily a positioning/descent hitch. For efficient SRT ascent, mechanical ascenders (Petzl Zigzag, Rope Wrench system, etc.) are more appropriate.

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

The shift toward SRT climbing in production arborist work is real and happening quickly. If you're doing high-volume climbing (large ornamentals, tall ponderosas), SRT with VT Hitch + Rope Wrench is faster and less physically demanding than DRT for ascent on larger trees. The investment: a spliced VT cord (about $30–40 from a supplier like Wesspur or SherrillTree), a compatible friction ring, and the practice hours to get the geometry right. The payoff is significant over a season of climbing.

⬡ Inspection & Retirement

  • Inspect the cord before every climb — look for sheath wear, flat spots, glazing (shiny, hardened surface from heat), or damage at the spliced eyes
  • Retire immediately if: glazed/heat-damaged surface, flat spots anywhere on the cord, sheath cuts exposing core, or loss of rope-hand feel (stiffness indicating internal damage)
  • Per ISA BMP — Rigging: friction hitch cords showing ≥10% diameter reduction or sheath damage must be retired
  • Log condition on inspections — keep inspection records with your gear
  • VT Hitch cords with spliced eyes: inspect the splice termination carefully — fraying or opening of the splice requires retirement
📜 ANSI Z133 Standard

Per ANSI Z133-2017: all climbing and work positioning equipment must be inspected before each use. Equipment found to be defective must be removed from service immediately. No friction hitch cord shall be used if it cannot reliably hold the climber's weight under normal operating conditions.
