🪚 The Prusik Hitch

A friction hitch that's been keeping arborists alive since Karl Prusik invented it for rope rescue in 1931. It's on your climbing system every day — this lesson covers why it works, how to rig it right, when it fails, and what the ANSI standard requires.

What the Prusik Is

Friction Hitch Fundamentals

A Prusik (also written "Prussik") is a friction hitch: a loop of cordage wrapped around a larger-diameter rope in a specific pattern. When loaded, the wraps grip the host rope through friction. When load is released, the hitch slides freely — allowing repositioning.

This combination of gripping under load and sliding when unloaded makes it useful for:

  • Safety backups on a primary climbing line (if you fall, it grips)
  • Controlled descents with a removable friction element
  • Ascending: foot loop + hand loop Prusik systems
  • Load transfer and rigging friction management

The Prusik is classified as a friction hitch — distinct from a stopper knot (terminal) or a bend (joining two ropes). Per ANSI Z133-2017 Section 9, all climbers using a stationary rope system (SRS) must have a friction hitch or mechanical backup device.

Tying the Classic Prusik
  1. Start with a Prusik loop — a length of cord joined into a loop with a double fisherman's knot. Hold the knot off to one side of the host rope.
  2. Pass the loop around the host rope and back through itself — creating the first pair of wraps. Pull snug.
  3. Pass the loop around the host rope again and back through itself a second time — three full passes total on each side of the host rope, six layers of cord in the grip zone.
  4. Dress the hitch so all wraps lie parallel and flat — no crossing or overlapping. Crossed wraps dramatically reduce grip strength.
  5. The knot joining the Prusik loop should sit outside the wraps, not inside the grip zone. Test: load the loop — hitch should grip immediately. Release — hitch should slide freely when pushed.
📋 Field Note

The most common Prusik failure in the field isn't the knot — it's dressing. Climbers tie three wraps quickly and the cord rolls or crosses. If any wrap is crossed, the effective grip surface is reduced and the hitch can slip under shock load. Take 10 extra seconds to dress it properly. Every time.

Cord-to-Rope Diameter Ratio

Why Ratio Matters

The Prusik works on friction — and friction is a function of the surface contact area between the cordage and the host rope. If the Prusik cord is too large relative to the host rope, it can't wrap tightly enough to grip. If it's too small, it can cut into the host rope fibers and generate uneven friction.

Arboricultural Applications
Primary Use

Safety Backup — SRS Climbing

Per ANSI Z133-2017 Section 9.1.5: when climbing on a stationary rope system, a secondary friction hitch or mechanical backup must be used. The Prusik on a foot loop is the classic solution — as the climber ascends, the Prusik is pushed up; if the primary system fails, the Prusik grips.

Common Use

Work Positioning Backup

A Prusik on the climbing line below the hitch climber (or Rope Wrench) provides a secondary holding point when working in position. Particularly important when performing cutting operations that could shock-load the primary hitch.

Advanced

Ascending — Foot Loop System

Two Prusik loops: one attached to a foot loop (foot Prusik), one to the harness (chest or back Prusik). Alternately weighting and sliding each allows ascending a fixed line. Slower than mechanical ascenders but requires no hardware beyond two Prusik loops.

Rigging

Load Transfer Friction

In rigging scenarios, a Prusik can provide a controllable friction point during load transfer. However, Prusik cord is not a rated rigging component — always verify that the Prusik's role in a rigging system is within the cord's MBS (minimum breaking strength), with appropriate safety factor per ANSI Z133.

When the Prusik Fails — And Why

The Four Failure Conditions

  • Heat glazing: Rapid uncontrolled descent generates heat through friction. If the descent is too fast, the Prusik cord's nylon fibers heat up and harden (glaze), dramatically reducing grip. Never use a Prusik for rapid or prolonged descent without managing descent speed. Glazed cord must be retired.
  • Wrong rope geometry: A Prusik tied on a rope of similar or larger diameter than the cord cannot generate enough friction to hold. Always check the ratio.
  • Poor dressing: Crossed or overlapping wraps reduce effective contact area. The hitch may hold until a sudden shock load — then slip.
  • Shock loading: A Prusik is not designed for fall arrest. A high fall factor with a Prusik backup can generate enough force to slide the hitch (partial arrest) or to overload the cord. The Prusik is a backup system — the primary system should prevent the fall scenario where the Prusik would be shock-loaded.
⚠ Do Not Use a Prusik:

On ropes of equal or greater diameter than the Prusik cord  ·  As a primary climbing system without a backup  ·  When the cord shows glazing, wear, or discolouration from heat  ·  In pulleys or through carabiners where it may not self-lock correctly  ·  As a fall arrest device in high fall-factor scenarios

📋 Field Note

Retire your Prusik cord on a set schedule — don't wait for visible failure. Nylon degrades from UV, heat cycles, abrasion, and loading even when you can't see it. Industry guidance is to retire climbing cordage annually under heavy use, or after any significant shock-loading event. The cord is cheap. The cost of a ground-level event is not.

Friction Hitch Variations
Classic

3-Wrap Prusik

Three passes around the host rope. Standard for most climbing applications. Symmetric — grips in both directions (up and down load). The go-to for most arborists.

Directional

Klemheist (French Prusik)

Cord wrapped helically around host rope, loop passed up through itself. More efficient to slide upward — preferred for foot ascender application. Easier to manage with one hand. Does NOT grip as efficiently in a downward direction, so must be oriented correctly.

Advanced

Machard Hitch

Similar to Klemheist but tied with the free end rather than a loop. Good for creating improvised Prusik-style hitches from a single strand. Directional — check orientation before trusting it with your life.

Mechanical

Mechanical Alternatives

The Hitch Climber (HC) system and rope wrenches (e.g., Teufelberger Rope Wrench) are mechanical friction devices that replace the Prusik in many SRS setups. They're faster and more consistent — but they're hardware, with inspection and retirement requirements. A Prusik requires no hardware and no batteries. Know both.
