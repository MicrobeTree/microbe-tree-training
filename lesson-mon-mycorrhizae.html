📝 Quiz — Work Through All Five

Question 1 of 5
At the cellular level, what is the defining structural difference between ectomycorrhizal (ECM) and arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi?

ECM vs AM — The Structural Distinction

The key difference is how far in the fungus gets. In ECM associations, the fungus wraps the root tip in a dense mantle of fungal tissue, then extends inward through the apoplast (the space between cells) to form the Hartig net — an intricate lattice of hyphae threading between cortical cells. The fungus never breaches the cell membrane. It's more like a scaffold built between bricks than drilling through them.

In AM associations, the fungus actually penetrates through the root epidermis and into cortical cells, where it forms highly branched tree-like structures called arbuscules (and sometimes balloon-like storage structures called vesicles). This intimate contact between fungal membrane and plant cell membrane is where the nutrient and carbon exchange happens.

  • ECM: mantle + Hartig net; hyphae stay in the apoplast (between cells)
  • AM: no mantle; hyphae enter cells and form arbuscules + vesicles
  • ECM visible externally — bloated, branched root tips with smooth, waxy appearance
  • AM invisible without microscopy — no visible root tip change
  • Both types extend hyphae far beyond the root zone — up to several metres — massively expanding the tree's effective absorptive surface area
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

When you're digging around the base of a ponderosa or lodgepole on site, look for the characteristic stubby, Y-shaped ECM root tips — often yellowish, white, or russet-coloured, and unusually thick compared to fine roots. That's the mantle. Damage those and you're cutting the tree's supply chain. Soil compaction, grade changes, and trenching in the drip zone destroy ECM networks. The ISA Study Guide (Ch. 5) specifically flags mycorrhizal disruption as a primary mechanism of construction-related tree decline. Per the ISA BMP for PHC, preserving existing soil biology is a first-line intervention before any inoculant product is considered.

Question 2 of 5
A client wants to establish three trees along their new fence line in Westbank: a silver maple, a green ash, and a ponderosa pine. Which of these is most likely to form ectomycorrhizal (ECM) associations?

Host Range — Knowing Which Tree Needs Which Fungus

This is practically important because mycorrhizal inoculant products are not interchangeable. Applying an ECM inoculant to a maple does nothing — maples form AM associations. Applying an AM inoculant to a pine is equally useless.

  • ECM hosts in Okanagan: All Pinus spp. (ponderosa, lodgepole, white pine), all Abies spp. (fir), Picea spp. (spruce), Pseudotsuga menziesii (Douglas-fir), Larix spp. (larch), Betula spp. (birch), Quercus spp. (oak), Alnus spp. (alder), Populus spp. (aspen, cottonwood)
  • AM hosts in Okanagan: All Acer spp. (maple), Fraxinus spp. (ash), Ulmus spp. (elm), Tilia spp. (linden), Malus spp. (apple/crabapple), Prunus spp. (cherry, plum), most herbaceous plants
  • Some tree genera form both types — willows (Salix) and poplars (Populus) can form ECM primarily, with AM present on some species
  • Soil pH matters: Okanagan's alkaline soils (pH 7.5–8.2) favour different fungal communities than acidic soils
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Green ash and silver maple — the workhorses of Okanagan boulevards — are AM hosts. When you see one of these trees in declining health following soil disturbance, the mycorrhizal component of that decline is AM disruption, not ECM. The practical takeaway: if a client asks about "mycorrhizal inoculants" for a newly planted ash or maple, they need an AM product containing Glomus/Rhizophagus species, not the ECM ectomycorrhizal products marketed for conifers. Read labels carefully — many products blend both types for this reason.

Question 3 of 5
A client's white birch has been declining for two seasons. Soil testing reveals severe compaction in the drip zone and pH 8.0. No pathogens are found. Which mechanism best explains how compaction impairs the tree's mycorrhizal function?

Compaction and Mycorrhizal Collapse — The Oxygen Connection

Mycorrhizal fungi are obligate aerobes. Their hyphae need oxygen to respire and function. Soil compaction — whether from foot traffic, vehicle parking, or grade changes — collapses macropores and eliminates the air spaces soil fungi depend on. The result is hypoxic conditions that are lethal to ECM hyphae even before the tree roots themselves are visibly affected.

  • Well-structured soil: 50% solid, 25% water, 25% air by volume (general target)
  • Compacted soil: pore space drops toward 10% or less; water-filled pores become anaerobic
  • ECM hyphae extend up to 10–15 cm depth in surface horizons — the exact zone most affected by surface compaction
  • Loss of ECM = loss of phosphorus and micronutrient uptake, loss of drought buffer, loss of pathogen protection
  • Alkaline pH (Okanagan's 7.5–8.2 soils) already limits phosphorus availability — compaction compounds this dramatically
  • Aeration techniques (vertical mulching, radial aeration, air spading) are the primary remediation per ISA BMP for Soil Management
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

This is one of the most common decline scenarios you'll see on Okanagan residential properties — a birch or spruce near a parking pad that's "just not doing well." The soil under that gravel pad is likely dead, biologically speaking. Air spading the root zone, applying organic mulch, and giving the tree a 2–3 year recovery timeline is the correct PHC approach. Document the compaction with a penetrometer reading before and after treatment so you can demonstrate improvement. Per ISA BMP for PHC, avoiding the source of stress (the compaction itself) takes priority over any supplemental treatment.

Question 4 of 5
Suzanne Simard's research in BC interior forests showed that large "mother trees" can transfer carbon to seedlings growing beneath them via common mycorrhizal networks (CMN). What is the most directly applicable implication for an arborist advising on a development project in the Okanagan interior?

The Common Mycorrhizal Network — Practical Arborist Implications

Simard's research — conducted primarily in interior BC Douglas-fir and birch forests — established that trees of different species are connected by shared ECM fungal networks. Carbon, water, and possibly stress signals move through these networks. Large, older trees ("mother trees") act as hubs, and their removal doesn't just take away one tree — it can destabilize the underground infrastructure supporting the entire stand.

  • CMN = Common Mycorrhizal Network — the connected hyphal web linking multiple trees through shared fungal partners
  • Carbon transfer from mother trees to shaded seedlings is significant enough to improve seedling survival measurably
  • Mycorrhizal inoculants do NOT replicate an established CMN — commercial products introduce fungi but don't restore network connectivity or the species diversity of a functioning forest floor community
  • Development tree preservation recommendations should explicitly note CMN disruption risk — this is ISA Tree Risk Assessment domain content
  • Per ISA BMP for PHC, "biological soil integrity" is identified as a component of site assessment before treatment planning
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

In the Okanagan interior, ponderosa pine stands operate as connected communities through Rhizopogon, Suillus, and other ECM fungi. When you advise a client on a land clearing project near an existing stand, the strongest tree-preservation argument isn't just about the visible canopy — it's about the invisible infrastructure under the soil. A mature ponderosa left at the edge of a development pad may support seedling regeneration and soil biology across the entire cleared zone, IF its root zone and CMN are not destroyed by compaction or trenching. Document this in your report to give it weight. Simard's primary research publications and her book Finding the Mother Tree (2021) are the key references if a client wants to see the science.

Question 5 of 5
An arborist is proposing a systemic fungicide trunk injection to manage a confirmed Dothistroma needle blight infection on a mature ponderosa pine. What mycorrhizal consideration should be documented in the pre-treatment assessment?

Systemic Fungicides and Mycorrhizal Risk

This is a real clinical tension in PHC. Systemic fungicides — azoxystrobin, propiconazole, phosphonate compounds — move through the xylem and phloem after injection or soil application. They are effective against target pathogens, but many of these molecules are not selective: they can suppress a range of fungal organisms, including the ECM fungi the tree depends on.

  • The active ECM hyphal network lives in the same feeder root tissue the fungicide reaches via xylem translocation
  • Phosphonate (phosphorous acid) products — widely used for Phytophthora and downy mildew — are documented to impair mycorrhizal colonization in some contexts
  • Propiconazole (DMI fungicide) has ECM suppression potential documented in forest research literature
  • The trade-off is real: treating the pathogen may weaken the mutualist — the net effect on tree health depends on severity of disease vs. degree of mycorrhizal suppression
  • Best practice per ISA BMP for PHC: document the trade-off, consider targeted application (trunk injection over soil drench to minimize root zone exposure), monitor ECM status post-treatment
  • ANSI Z133 sets no specific fungicide–mycorrhizae exclusion zones — option C is invented
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

In practice, if you're treating a Dothistroma-infected ponderosa with a systemic fungicide by trunk injection, the decision is usually justified — severe Dothistroma defoliation is a serious stressor that will reduce the tree's carbohydrate output and actually impair mycorrhizal function anyway. The lesson isn't "don't treat." It's "document the trade-off and choose the most targeted delivery method." Trunk injection concentrates the product in the canopy where Dothistroma lives, which is safer for root zone fungi than a soil drench. This is the kind of nuanced PHC reasoning the ISA CA exam tests in the Soil Management and Tree Biology domains — not just "what's the treatment" but "what are the second-order effects."
