Limb Removal Sequencing & Beam Mechanics

The three-cut method exists for one reason: a limb under its own weight behaves like a beam. Understand tension and compression and you stop bark tears, pinched bars and surprises.

A Limb Is a Loaded Beam

A horizontal limb hanging off a stem works like a cantilever beam. Its own weight stretches the top (tension) and squeezes the bottom (compression).

What that means for your saw

  • Cut into the tension side (top) first, and the limb sags as the kerf opens. Before the wood finishes, it can tear down the underside, stripping bark back toward the stem.
  • Cut into the compression side (bottom) and the kerf closes and pinches the bar if you go too deep.
  • So: a shallow undercut to stop the tear, then a top cut to let it go.
📋 Field Note — Microbe Tree Crew

Read every limb before you cut: which way it's loaded, where it will go, and whether anything (a rope, a wire, a roof) changes the picture.

The Three-Cut Method

  1. Undercut: on the underside, a short distance out from the branch collar. Go in only part-way; the ISA Study Guide describes about ⅛–¼ of the diameter for a drop cut. Its job is to stop a bark tear running back to the stem.
  2. Top cut: from above, directly over or just outboard of the undercut. The limb drops cleanly. A top cut set too far out can make the limb pivot and grab or pull the saw.
  3. Final cut: remove the stub just outside the branch collar and branch bark ridge, leaving a clean surface with the bark firmly attached.
📐 ANSI A300 (Part 1, Pruning): branch removal cuts are made without cutting into the branch bark ridge or collar and without leaving a stub. Flush cuts are not acceptable. Branches too heavy to support with one hand should be pre-cut to prevent splitting or bark tearing.

When the Load Changes

Upright or leaning limbs

Lean shifts where tension and compression sit. On a steep leaning stem or limb, the side away from the lean is in tension. Fast, deep cuts on heavy leaners risk a barber chair (the piece splits up the stem). Cut efficiently and keep clear of the split path.

Bound or pinned limbs

A limb resting on a roof, another limb or the ground can be reverse-loaded, with compression on top. Look for where it's supported before you cut, and expect the kerf to behave differently.

Rigged limbs

A tight rigging line changes the forces: the limb may lift, swing or hinge instead of dropping. Keep the slack out, agree on the plan with the ground crew, and plan the cut for where the rope will take the piece, not where gravity alone would.

Hinge / notch cuts

For steering a limb or a top, use felling-style cuts (notch plus back cut) so the hinge controls the direction. Covered in our notch geometry and controlled lowering lessons.

Cutting Aloft

📐 ANSI Z133-2017 §6.3.6: arborists shall be tied in and use a second means of being secured (e.g., a work-positioning lanyard or second climbing line) when operating a chainsaw in a tree. Both ends of one two-in-one lanyard don't count as two.
  • Position so the limb can't swing or kick back into you. Work from the side or above, never directly under the piece.
  • Know where your climbing line and lanyard are at every cut. Keep them out of the saw's path and off the piece being cut.
  • Both hands on the saw. Check that the chain brake works and that your PPE (helmet, eye and hearing protection, saw-resistant leg protection as required) is on.
  • When you reposition, preload the new tie-in with your full weight before releasing the old one (Z133 §8.1.6).
  • Call the cut. Wait for the ground crew to confirm the drop zone is clear.
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Ponderosa and Douglas-fir limbs on our dry sites are often heavy, brittle or dead-tipped. Dead wood doesn't bend before it lets go. Take smaller pieces and expect less warning.

Tailgate Quick Check

  1. Point to a limb: where's the tension side? Is anything supporting or binding it?
  2. Walk through the three cuts for that limb. Where does each go?
  3. Where's the final cut relative to the collar and branch bark ridge?
  4. Climber: what are your two means of being secured when the saw is running?
  5. Ground: who watches the drop zone, and what's the call before each cut?
