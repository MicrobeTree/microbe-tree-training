🪚 Equipment, Tools & Knots — Week 10

The Figure-8 Family

The figure-8 isn't one knot — it's a family. The stopper, the bight, the follow-through. They share a shape and a principle: the double loop creates a stable, load-resistant structure that's easy to inspect and won't jamb like an overhand after a hard load. Know all three. Know when each one is right.

Figure-8 Stopper
Also: Flemish knot · Savoy knot
Primary Use

Tail-end stopper — prevents rope from running through a device (friction hitch, pulley, ring). Replaces the overhand stopper where security and inspectability matter.

Figure-8 on a Bight
Also: Figure-8 loop · Flemish loop
Primary Use

Fixed loop tied in the end or middle of a rope — for clipping into a carabiner, attaching a block, or creating an anchor point. Can be tied anywhere in the rope length.

Figure-8 Follow-Through
Also: Retrace · Threaded figure-8
Primary Use

Ties a figure-8 loop directly around an anchor, harness tie-in point, or object where a carabiner cannot be used. The trace follows the original knot exactly.

📐 How to Tie Each Knot

Figure-8 Stopper
1
Hold the working end of the rope. Make a loop by crossing the working end over the standing part — the tail goes under and behind.
2
Pass the working end back through the loop from underneath — same direction as you'd tie an overhand, but keep going.
3
Route the working end over the standing part and back down through the original loop — you'll see the figure-8 shape forming.
4
Dress the knot: tighten both loops symmetrically. Working end should exit the knot in the same direction as the standing part. Leave a tail of at least 10× the rope diameter.
Figure-8 on a Bight
1
Double back approximately 60cm of rope to create a bight (U-fold). Hold the bight at the fold — you're now working with a doubled strand.
2
With the doubled strand as your "working end," tie a figure-8 stopper knot: loop over, under, then through.
3
The folded end (bight) of the rope forms the finished loop. Dress symmetrically — both strands of the loop should run parallel with no crossover.
4
Verify: the knot has the correct figure-8 shape on both sides. The loop should be the right size for your carabiner or application. Adjust before loading.
Figure-8 Follow-Through
1
Tie a single-strand figure-8 stopper knot loosely in the working end, leaving enough tail to complete the retrace (at least 50cm for a harness tie-in).
2
Thread the working end through or around your anchor point (harness tie-in loop, ring, or object). The end comes back toward the knot.
3
Retrace: follow the original knot's path exactly in reverse, threading the working end alongside — gever crossing — the original strands. Your tail exits parallel to the standing part.
4
Dress and set: both loop strands should be parallel and snug, no crossing. Tail minimum 10× rope diameter. Inspect — you should see a perfect doubled figure-8 shape.
🔍 Visual Inspection Check
✓
The finished knot shows two distinct loops forming the figure-8 shape on both sides. No twisted or crossed strands inside the knot.
✓
All strands are parallel throughout — especially critical in the follow-through. Crossed strands reduce knot strength significantly.
✓
Working tail exits in the correct direction with adequate length. A short tail that's just past the knot can slip through under load.
✓
Knot is fully dressed and set — got just loosely formed. All slack removed before loading. "Dress before you stress" is a simple rule worth keeping.

⚖️ Load Characteristics & Comparison

All knots reduce the working strength of a rope. The mechanism is stress concentration at the bend points inside the knot. Tighter radii = more stress concentration = greater strength reduction. The figure-8 family outperforms the overhand family because the figure-8 geometry distributes load across more material with gentler bends.

⚠ Critical Safety Note — ANSI Z133

Per ANSI Z133 §7.4.1: "All knots, hitches, and other connections used in tree care operations shall be appropriate for the intended use, shall be properly dressed, and shall be inspected before each use." A figure-8 that has not been properly dressed — parallel strands, correct shape, adequate tail — does not meet this standard regardless of what knot it was intended to be. Dress before you stress, inspect before you climb.

🚫 When NOT to Use a Figure-8

  • Dynamic shock loading: The figure-8 is a static knot family. It's not designed to absorb shock loads (sudden falls). In a dynamic fall arrest system, purpose-built connectors and rated arrest lanyards are required per ANSI Z133 §7 and WorkSafeBC Part 11. The figure-8 has no energy-absorbing properties.
  • Mid-rope position where load comes from both sides: The figure-8 on a bight can be loaded from both legs symmetrically, but if one leg takes dramatically more load than the other (e.g. during directional rigging), the knot can shift. Prefer a rated sewn sling or anchor strap in such applications.
  • As the sole attachment in a multi-point rigging system: Any single knot connection should be backed up with a secondary attachment where possible, per the general safe work practice of redundancy in life-safety systems.
  • On severely worn or damaged rope: No knot — including a figure-8 — compensates for rope degradation. Inspect your rope end-to-end before tying. A figure-8 on a section of rope with internal core damage is not a safe connection.
  • In place of a rated mechanical connector when one is required: Certain applications per ANSI Z133 and manufacturer specifications require rated mechanical hardware (carabiners, rings, shackles). A knot does not substitute for a rated connector in those contexts.
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

On a tight strata site in Kelowna — say a co-dominant Siberian elm between two houses — you might set a rigging point with a figure-8 on a bight to attach your block, then use a figure-8 follow-through on your climbing line to harness if your system requires it. When your lead asks you to show your connection before going up, they want to see the whole figure-8 shape visible, strands parallel, and a decent tail. If it looks like a blob, re-tie it. This is one of the most common informal checks a good crew leader runs — and it's worth doing to each other, not just expecting the new person to get it right on their own. Per ANSI Z133 §4.4: supervisors are responsible for verifying that workers are using proper equipment and techniques.
