Western Tent Caterpillar (Malacosoma californicum)

Spring tents look alarming and get clients calling. Know the biology, tell it apart from look-alikes, and match the response to the tree's real risk.

🐛 Fast facts

Malacosoma californicum (western tent caterpillar)
One per year
As eggs in a band around a twig
Willow, cottonwood, aspen, alder, birch, wild rose, chokecherry, apple, cherry, plum, hawthorn

Big, ugly tents in spring set off a lot of client calls. The arborist's job is telling a healthy tree that will bounce back from one that needs help.

Question 1 of 5
Where and how does western tent caterpillar spend the winter?
Egg bands: your winter control window

Each female lays one egg band of roughly 150–250 eggs around a small twig, covered in a frothy, hardened coating called spumaline (NRCan; USDA FS FIDL 119). The larvae actually form inside the eggs within weeks but stay put until spring, when hatch is timed to budbreak on the host.

Egg bands are often on the sunny (south/southwest) side of open-grown host trees and on twigs thinner than a pencil to about 2 cm.

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Dormant-season pruning on fruit trees, willows and hawthorns is the perfect time to look for egg bands. Snip them off and bag them. It's the cheapest control there is, and clients love seeing what you found.

Question 2 of 5
A client calls in May about silk tents in the crotches of their crabapple. In late August, another client calls about loose webbing enclosing leaves at the branch tips of a chokecherry. What's the most likely pair?
Timing and tent position tell them apart
  • Western tent caterpillar: spring tents (right after budbreak), built in branch crotches and forks. Larvae leave the tent to feed and come back to rest.
  • Fall webworm (Hyphantria cunea): late summer webs that enclose foliage at the ends of branches. Larvae feed inside the web.
  • Forest tent caterpillar (M. disstria) doesn't build a true tent. Larvae rest in silk mats on trunks and branches, and outbreaks hit trembling aspen stands.

Getting the ID right sets the timing of any treatment and the advice you give the client.

Question 3 of 5
What best describes the impact of one season of heavy tent caterpillar defoliation on a healthy, established aspen or willow?
Defoliation draws down the reserves

Larvae feed for about 4–6 weeks in spring (USDA FS: 30–42 days, depending on weather), then pupate. Healthy deciduous trees usually push a second flush of leaves the same season, paid for by stored carbohydrates (remember the NSC lesson).

The concern is stacking stresses: several years of defoliation, drought, root damage or a young transplant. Each refoliation drains reserves the tree would otherwise use for defence and winter hardiness, which opens the door to cankers and borers.

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

On our dry sites, a defoliated tree that's also been short on water all summer is the one to watch. Prioritize treating newly planted and stressed ornamentals. Healthy mature cottonwoods along the creek can usually ride it out.

Question 4 of 5
Western tent caterpillar populations in BC are known to:
Boom and bust

Long-term studies in southwestern BC recorded western tent caterpillar populations peaking every 6–11 years (Myers 2000). Outbreaks typically collapse on their own, with disease (including a virus) and parasitoids and predators playing major roles.

That matters for advice: in an outbreak year, the population is often close to crashing anyway. Treating every tent on every wild tree isn't sensible. Focus on high-value and stressed trees.

Exact cycle timing in the Okanagan isn't well documented. Watch local reports rather than predicting.

Question 5 of 5
For a stressed ornamental crabapple with several active tents in early spring, which IPM approach fits best?
Mechanical first, selective product second
  • Mechanical: prune out or pull down tents with larvae inside, early morning or evening when they're home. Bag them.
  • Biological: Bacillus thuringiensis var. kurstaki (Btk) targets caterpillars and works best on young larvae that are actively feeding. It's selective, so it spares many beneficial insects.
  • Never burn tents in trees. It damages bark and cambium and is a fire risk, especially in the dry Okanagan.
📐 In BC, pesticide use is regulated under the Integrated Pest Management Act. Commercial applications need the appropriate licence and certified applicators, and always follow the product label.
📋 Field Note — Crew

Caterpillar hairs and silk can irritate skin and eyes. Wear gloves and eye protection when pulling tents, and wash up before eating.
