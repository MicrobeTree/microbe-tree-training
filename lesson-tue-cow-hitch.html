EQUIPMENT & KNOTS — Week 14, Cow Hitch & Girth Hitch

The cow hitch is the simplest hitch in the arborist toolkit — and the most frequently misused. It's everywhere: friction savers, eye slings, lanyard terminations, hardware attachment. Get it right and it's solid. Get it wrong (or use it where you shouldn't) and it can roll off a smooth anchor or create an eccentric load that surprises you at height. This lesson covers the geometry, the grip conditions, and when to reach for something else instead.

→ Work through it. Field note at the end of each section.

Cow Hitch (Lark's Head)

The cow hitch — also called the lark's head or ring hitch — is a two-loop hitch formed by passing a bight through a ring or around an anchor, then passing the two ends (or working loops) back through the bight. It grips in two opposite directions simultaneously, making it a symmetrical, bidirectional anchor hitch.

📋 Field Note

You'll tie a cow hitch every day without thinking about it — every time you attach a friction saver to a limb, girth-hitch a lanyard through a ring, or mount a rigging sling through a carabiner. Understanding its geometry tells you when it's the right choice and when to think twice.

Tying the Cow Hitch

Cow hitch through a ring — both loops over the anchor, load pulls bight

Method 1 — Through a Ring (Closed Anchor)

  1. Pass the bight of your sling or cord through the ring from front to back.
  2. Open the bight and pass it back over the ring so both legs of the bight straddle the ring's circumference.
  3. Pull the bight down — the two loops tighten onto the ring from both sides.
  4. Dress neatly — both loops should lie flat and parallel on the ring; no twisting.

Method 2 — Around a Limb or Post (Open Anchor)

  1. Cross the two ends (or loops) of your sling behind the anchor point to form an X.
  2. Bring each end around in front of the anchor, passing them through the opposite loop.
  3. Load the bight — the crossing point tightens onto the anchor as tension is applied.
⚠ Critical Dressing Point

Both loops must contact the anchor symmetrically. A twisted or unbalanced cow hitch concentrates load on one side — it can roll off a smooth anchor or cause the attachment point to shift. Dress before loading. Inspect before climbing.

📋 Field Note

The friction saver attachment at the top of your climbing system is typically a cow hitch or a girth hitch around the limb. Before every climb, check that it's dressed correctly — particularly after repositioning. A misaligned cow hitch on a friction saver can creep toward the end of the limb under repeated loading, especially on smooth-barked species like beech or younger maple. This is not hypothetical; it's a documented incident cause.

Working Load Limit Warning — Girth-Hitching

When you girth-hitch (cow hitch) an eye sling through a ring or carabiner, the sling's rated WLL is significantly reduced. The sharp bend radius at the hitch concentrates stress on the sling material. ISA BMP for Rigging notes that choker-hitch (similar geometry) can reduce WLL by 50–70%. Always consult the sling manufacturer's data for specific reduction factors — do not assume the rated WLL applies to choked configurations.

📋 Field Note — Rigging Math

If you have a 2:1 rigging system and your eye sling is girth-hitched to the rigging ring, and the sling's straight-pull WLL is 6,000 lbs, your actual safe working capacity at that attachment point might be closer to 3,000 lbs. Then factor in dynamic loads from cutting and you have a narrow margin. This is why rigging assessments — not just 'it looks fine' — are required before large piece removals per ANSI Z133 §9.

Girth Hitch — The Cow Hitch's Close Relative

The girth hitch (also called a larkshead when formed from a loop) is functionally nearly identical to the cow hitch in many arborist applications — the terms are often used interchangeably in the field. The distinction:

NameHow FormedResult
Cow HitchBight passed through anchor, opened over anchorTwo separate strands straddling the anchor
Girth HitchOne loop of a closed sling passed through itself around an anchorLooks same as cow hitch; loop tightens onto anchor
Lark's HeadSame as girth hitch; traditional nameSame

For practical purposes: if you take a sewn eye sling and pass one eye through the other around a post, you've tied a girth hitch. If you take a bight of rope through a ring and open it over the ring, you've tied a cow hitch. The loading geometry and cautions are the same.

When NOT to Use the Cow Hitch

⛔Avoid In These Situations
  • Smooth, round anchors with no lip or cleat: A cow hitch can roll and slip along a smooth rope or pole under unequal loading. Use a half hitch or additional securing wrap if the anchor is smooth and cylindrical.
  • Unbalanced loading: If one leg of the cow hitch will be loaded significantly more than the other, the hitch is asymmetrically stressed and may roll. In this case, a bowline or figure-8 on a bight is more appropriate.
  • As a primary life-safety connection without carabiner: A cow hitch alone around a limb (without carabiner) is not a rated attachment — use with appropriate hardware.
  • On sharp-edged anchors: The bend radius at the hitch concentrates stress — sharp metal edges or bark corners can damage the sling material. Use edge protection or a different attachment method.
  • When WLL reduction cannot be accommodated: If your rigging math requires the sling's full straight-pull WLL, do not girth-hitch — use a shackle or proper rigging ring instead.

Per ANSI Z133 §9.2: "All rigging equipment shall be inspected before each use and shall not be used if damaged or if there is any doubt about its condition." A cow hitch that has slipped or abraded during a job requires inspection before further use — inspect the sling at the hitch point for heat damage, abrasion, or glazing.

📋 Field Note

In the Okanagan summer, heat from sun-baked slings combined with repeated cow hitch loading can degrade synthetic sling material faster than you'd expect. Polyester degrades more slowly in UV than nylon, but both lose strength with heat and mechanical abrasion. Run your thumb along the sling where it contacts the hitch point after a hot rigging day — rough, stiff, or glazed texture means retire it. A sling that costs $30 is not worth the risk of an attachment failure 20 metres up.
