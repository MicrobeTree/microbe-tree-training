🥶 Cold, wet and early dark

November in the Okanagan means frosty mornings, wet bark, short days and tired hands. The risks stack up: slips on iced decks, branches and ladders, cold hands that can't tie a knot or run a saw properly, cold stress that creeps up without you noticing, and daylight running out before the job is done. None of it is dramatic until someone makes a mistake.

Rule of thumbUSFS National Tree Climbing Guide: "Do not start a tree climb that cannot be completed in full daylight." That's a US Forest Service guide, not BC law, but it is a good rule for us.

📋 What WorkSafeBC says about cold

  • Five causes of cold stress (WorkSafeBC): cold environments, wind, wet clothing (sweat or water), cold-water immersion, and fatigue.
  • Feeling cold is the key warning sign: it means you're losing heat faster than you make it. Hypothermia is gradual, so workers may not realize until it's too late.
  • Warning signs: mild = shivering, grogginess, poor judgment; moderate = violent shivering, can't think or pay attention, slurred speech, poor coordination; severe = loss of consciousness (call 911).
  • OHS Regulation, Part 7 (online version): employer must do a cold stress assessment and exposure control plan (7.34); workers must wear adequate insulating clothing and PPE, plus eye protection in snow or ice terrain with glare or blowing ice crystals (7.37); a worker showing cold-stress signs must be removed from exposure and treated by first aid or a physician (7.38).
  • Heated shelter (7.36): if the equivalent chill temperature is below −7°C (19°F), a nearby heated shelter must be available.
Question 1 of 5
A frosty 7:30 AM start at a West Kelowna job: the truck deck, ladder rungs and the first branches you'll climb are glazed with frost. What's the right approach?
Frost is a slip hazard
  • TCIA: de-ice and clear snow from equipment, especially where employees stand; use anti-slip material; salt or sand icy areas; wear boots with rough treads and/or added grips.
  • USFS National Tree Climbing Guide: rain-, ice- or snow-covered branches pose slipping hazards and climbers may need a safety line or lanyard at all times; do not climb wet ladders or ladders covered with ice or snow, and footwear should have rubber or neoprene soles and be dry.
📋 Field Note: Microbe Tree Crew

Frost lasts longest in shade. Check the footing at each stand, not just at the truck.

Question 2 of 5
Your fingers are numb and clumsy while setting a knot at 3°C in drizzle. What's the best fix?
Dexterity is a safety issue
  • USFS: cold impairs dexterity, especially in the fingers, which can jeopardize your ability to accomplish tasks safely, and conditions like air temperature "low enough to create an unsafe condition" are reasons not to climb unless mitigated in the job hazard analysis.
  • TCIA: studies vary on gloves versus mittens, but mittens appear better at lower temperatures; avoid bare hands if thin gloves still allow fine dexterity, and use them as a layer beneath mittens. Tight boots or saddles can limit blood flow to the extremities.
  • WorkSafeBC: ask whether tools and machines can be run without removing gloves or mittens.
Question 3 of 5
At 2 PM a crewmate working with you is shivering hard, can't follow instructions, slurs his words and fumbles his gear. What do you do?
Remove from exposure
  • WorkSafeBC: moderate hypothermia signs include violent shivering, inability to think or pay attention, slurred speech and poor body coordination. Mild signs: shivering, grogginess, poor judgment.
  • OHS Regulation 7.38 (online version): a worker who shows signs or reports symptoms of cold stress or injury must be removed from further exposure and treated by an appropriate first aid attendant, if available, or a physician.
  • The danger is that confusion and poor judgment make the person bad at noticing it. Watch each other. Call 911 for loss of consciousness or no breathing.
📋 Field Note: Microbe Tree Crew

Wet clothing and fatigue are two of the five causes. A cold crew member in a soaked base layer is not a "grit" problem.

Question 4 of 5
Which statement about heated shelters, wet clothing and wind is correct?
It's the chill, not just the air temp
  • OHS Reg 7.36: equivalent chill temperature less than −7°C (19°F), determined using the ACGIH cold stress criteria for wind chill, means a nearby heated shelter must be available.
  • CCOHS: if skin is wet and exposed to wind, the air temperature used for the wind chill table should be 10°C lower than actual. Wetness also reduces clothing insulation.
  • OHS Reg 7.34: the employer must do a cold stress assessment and an exposure control plan; 7.35 requires engineering controls first, then administrative controls or equally effective PPE.
  • Note: WorkSafeBC says the online regulation isn't the official version. The point for us is the principle: check the chill, not just the thermometer.
Question 5 of 5
It's 1:45 PM on Thursday, Nov 5 (sunset 5:28 PM). A client wants one more climbing removal that will take about 2.5 hours with rigging and cleanup, and the crew rule is back in the yard at 3:30 PM. What's the right call?
Plan back from sunset
  • Work backwards: a 2.5 hour job from 1:45 PM ends about 4:15 PM, which blows the 3:30 PM yard return. Sunset on Nov 5 is 5:28 PM (timeanddate), civil twilight ends about 6:02 PM; but you'd still be cleaning, loading and driving, tired and cold.
  • USFS: "Do not start a tree climb that cannot be completed in full daylight." The guide notes visibility matters especially late in the day when fatigue is a factor.
