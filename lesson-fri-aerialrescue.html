Worksite Techniques — Aerial Rescue

Friday Week 4 Microbe Tree Inc. · West Kelowna, BC

⚠ ANSI Z133 Requirement — Read This First

Per ANSI Z133 Section 10.1: Every worker who climbs trees must be trained and competent in aerial rescue procedures before ascending. This is not optional. It is a mandatory pre-qualification for climbing. If you have not practiced aerial rescue recently, you are not fully compliant with the standard — and your crew cannot safely conduct climbing operations without a qualified rescuer on the ground.

🎯 What Is Aerial Rescue & When Is It Needed?

Aerial rescue is the procedure for removing an injured, incapacitated, or unconscious climber from a tree. It is required when a climber:

  • Loses consciousness in the tree (medical emergency, impact, electrical contact)
  • Becomes entangled in rigging or gear and cannot self-rescue
  • Drops their climbing system and cannot descend
  • Is rendered incapable of descending by injury (broken bone, severe laceration, seizure)

Time is critical. Suspension trauma (also called harness-induced pathology) can develop in as little as 5–15 minutes in a suspended, unconscious victim. Blood pools in the legs; cardiac and respiratory function are compromised. A suspended, unresponsive climber is a medical emergency from minute one.

ANSI Z133 Section 10.2 — Suspension Trauma: "When a worker is found suspended and unconscious, suspension trauma shall be suspected and emergency rescue procedures shall be implemented immediately." The faster you get the person to the ground and into recovery position, the better the outcome.

📋 Rescue Response Sequence

1

SOUND THE ALARM

Announce emergency clearly. Assign one person to call 911 immediately. In the Okanagan, EMS response times to rural or hillside properties can be 10–20+ minutes. The call goes in first, before rescue begins. Give the dispatcher the address, describe the situation (worker suspended in tree), and report any known injuries.

2

ASSESS SCENE SAFETY

Do not rush in. A second casualty does not help the first. Assess:

  • Is the tree structurally stable? (No risk of further failure while you ascend?)
  • Are any power lines or sigging under dangerous tension?
  • Is the casualty still at risk from falling objects, active chainsaw, or running equipment?
  • Secure the scene — shut down equipment, clear bystanders, establish a safety perimeter.
3

DETERMINE RESCUE METHOD

Choose based on situation, casualty position, and available gear. Two primary methods:

Method A — Ground-Based Lower

Attach a lowering line to the casualty's harness. Use a mechanical advantage system (3:1 or 5:1) to control descent. Rescuer may not need to ascend if position allows.

  • Requires stable anchor above casualty
  • Requires access to a running end or ability to throw a line
  • Fastest if geometry allows

Method B — Pick-Off Rescue

Rescuer ascends adjacent to casualty, attaches to casualty's harness, transfers casualty's weight onto rescuer's system, and descends together.

  • Required when no ground-based lower is possible
  • Requires rescuer competency with tandem descent
  • More time — but often the only option
4

EXECUTE RESCUE — Key Principles

  • Never detach the casualty from the tree until a lowering system is rigged and under control
  • Maintain verbal contact with the casualty at all times if conscious
  • Control the descent rate — a free-fall, even short, can be fatal
  • For pick-off: attach to casualty's harness chest/dorsal attachment point with a rated connector before transferring weight
  • Use a separate friction device for controlled descent — do NOT rely on the casualty's descender if they are unconscious
5

LOWER TO GROUND & FIRST AID

  • Lower to a flat, clear area where first aid can be administered
  • Do not lay the casualty flat immediately if they have been suspended for more than a few minutes — risk of reflow syndrome. Per ANSI Z133 and WorkSafeBC guidance, place in a seated or semi-recumbent position for 30 minutes if conscious; follow EMS instructions if unconscious
  • Begin CPR/AED if no pulse or breathing
  • Treat for shock — keep warm, calm, reassured
  • Do not remove the harness (it may be providing compression to injured tissue)
  • Transfer to EMS on arrival and provide a full account of the mechanism of injury, suspension duration, and any interventions performed

⚙️ Rescue Kit — What Every Crew Needs On Site

Per ANSI Z133 Section 10.3, rescue equipment must be on site any time climbing operations are conducted. At minimum:

  • Rescue/lowering rope — dedicated, not the primary climbing rope. Minimum 15 m (50 ft) or sufficient for the tallest tree on site
  • Friction device capable of controlled lowering (figure-8, ATC, or friction hitch on a steel ring)
  • Rated connectors (locking carabiners, minimum 3 kN gate-closed rating) — at least 3 extra
  • Prussik loops or mechanical ascenders for the pick-off scenario (rescuer may need to ascend quickly)
  • First aid kit — WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation Part 3 specifies minimum contents for field work sites
  • Communication device — cell phone confirmed working for the site, or two-way radio with a charged base unit
  • Posted emergency plan — address of the work site, nearest cross street, hospital location, EMS number. Do not rely on memory in an emergency.

⚠ Do not use a climbing system for rescue that has been loaded to near-capacity or has been subject to shock loading. If you used your climbing rope in a rigging scenario earlier in the day, it is not appropriate for aerial rescue without inspection. Keep a dedicated rescue line in a separate bag, inspected and ready.

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

On Okanagan job sites — especially the estate properties above Lakeshore Drive, the orchard removal jobs in East Kelowna, or any acreage outside the Westbank town centre — your EMS response time is not 8 minutes. It can be 15–25 minutes. That changes everything about how you think about aerial rescue. The time it takes EMS to arrive is time your suspended casualty has been hanging. Your crew is the first responder. This is why we practice. Get your aerial rescue drills in before the climbing season. This Friday lesson is the starting point — the drill is where it actually gets learned.

�M Pre-Job Rescue Planning — Do This Every Day

ANSI Z133 and WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation both require a documented pre-job safety plan that includes emergency procedures. Before any climbing operation:

  • Designate a rescue-qualified crew member as the on-site rescuer for the day. This person stays on the ground when a climber is aloft. (On a 2-person crew, this is the groundworker.)
  • Walk the site and locate the address — confirm the correct civic address for EMS dispatch. On rural properties with non-standard addressing or long driveways, note the nearest cross street and GPS coordinates if cell signal is reliable.
  • Check cell signal at the work site before the climber goes up. If there's no reliable signal, establish a communication alternative.
  • Brief the rescue plan at tailgate: "If [climber] is incapacitated, [groundworker] calls 911, retrieves the rescue kit from [location], and executes [rescue method A or B] depending on position."
  • Identify the nearest hospital with trauma capability: Kelowna General Hospital, 2268 Pandosy St, Kelowna — (250) 862-4000. For Westside properties: drive time is approximately 15 minutes to KGH. EAP should note this.
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

The best aerial rescue is the one you never need. But if you need one and haven't practiced it, someone's life is in the hands of a crew running on adrenaline and half-remembered training. Schedule a practice rescue at least annually — ideally at the start of the climbing season in April. Use a rescue dummy or have a crew member act as the casualty. Time yourselves from alarm to casualty on the ground. You want that number under 4 minutes. If you don't know what your number is, you don't know if you're ready.
