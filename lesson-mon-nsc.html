Week 9 — NSC Reserves & Carbon Storage

Where trees store energy, why it matters for drought survival, wound response, and recovery from pruning — and what "carbohydrate depletion" means when you're standing in front of a declining tree.

📋 Quick Reference — NSC Reserves

What are NSCs? Soluble sugars (sucrose, glucose, fructose) + starch. NOT cell wall material. The tree's "energy bank account."
Where stored? Ray parenchyma, axial parenchyma, root cortex, living bark. NOT in heartwood — dead tissue, zero storage capacity.
Role in wound response CODIT walls, woundwood, resin synthesis, and antifungal compounds all require NSC. Low NSC = poor wound response.
Role in spring growth Trees draw on stored NSC to fund leaf-out before new leaves are photosynthetically active. Starch → sugar for bud break and shoot elongation.
Drought & NSC Drought reduces photosynthesis → NSC accumulates more slowly. Prolonged drought depletes reserves while respiration continues. "Carbon starvation" theory.
Pruning implication Removing >25% live crown reduces source tissue → slower NSC recovery. Heavy pruning during drought = double stress. ISA BMP: 25% max per cycle.

    Lesson Complete — NSC Reserves

    Week 9, Monday · Tree Biology

    You've worked through all five questions on NSC reserves. The mental model to keep: a tree's NSC is its energy account, and every stress, wound, and growth demand is a withdrawal. Healthy trees run a surplus; stressed trees run a deficit. When a stressed tree finally collapses, it's often not the last stressor that killed it — it's that the account hit zero.
