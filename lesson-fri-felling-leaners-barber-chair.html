Felling Leaners Without a Barber Chair

Monday showed why a leaner is a loaded spring. Today: how to release it in the right order, from the right side, and when to walk away.

⏱ About 10 minutes · Week 20 · Microbe Tree Inc. crew training

⚠️ The short answer

Monday's lesson: a leaning stem is a loaded spring, with the back (upper) side in tension. Cut a heavy leaner the normal way and it can fall early and split straight up the stem. The butt flies up and back along your escape route. That's a barber chair. The fix is to control the order the fibres let go: deep undercut, bore in, set the holding wood, and leave a backstrap that you cut last from a safe position.

STOP and get qualified help if you seeAdvanced decay in the stem or roots · a lifted root mat · no adequate escape route · poor footing · extensive splitting already · nowhere for the tree to go · a heavy uphill lean. (BCFSC Heavy Leaner SWP)
📐 This lesson uses the BC Forest Safety Council Heavy Leaner Bore Cut Safe Work Procedure (May 2026), developed by subject-matter experts from forestry, wildfire, oil & gas and WorkSafeBC. It's written for hand fallers. On our urban removals the same principles apply; if the tree or the site is beyond your training, we rig it down, use a machine, or bring in a certified faller.
Question 1 of 5
What exactly is a barber chair?
The split that comes back at you

BCFSC: a heavy leaner cut the traditional way may fall prematurely and/or split vertically. If it splits, the butt can move up and back along the escape route. The root plate can also lift and cut you off from your escape.

Other heavy-leaner hazards on the SWP list: loss of directional control, root pull, debris thrown back, bar pinched in the undercut, and early, rapid fall.

Question 2 of 5
Before any cut on a heavy leaner, what does the SWP require?
Ask why it's leaning
  • Assess top down: rotten top, hung-up or broken branches, fungus, and whether the top has corrected.
  • Look for cracks in the ground around the base and decay or conks on the roots. Check stem integrity, and test root stability visually and with an axe.
  • BCFSC: a recent lean can mean a compromised root system or soil that isn't holding the tree.
  • Escape routes must be long enough to clear a pulling root mat.
📋 Monday tie-in

A corrected top and reaction wood mean an old lean the tree has adapted to. A straight stem tipped over with heaving soil means fresh root failure. Very different trees to cut.

Question 3 of 5
How should you build the undercut on a heavy leaner?
Deep, slightly off the lean
  • BCFSC: undercut as deep as the tree will allow, preferably up to 25% of the tree diameter. That takes tension off the backstrap and the root system.
  • Aim it slightly off the lean to help avoid root pull and splitting.
  • Preferred opening: 1-to-1.
  • Heavy leaners pinch the bar in the undercut. Move the saw in the cut to feel for it.
Question 4 of 5
In the bore-in method on a medium tree, what is the backstrap and when is it cut?
Backstrap last

BCFSC bore-in steps (medium diameter, under bar length):

  1. Deep undercut, as above.
  2. Bore through the centre from the high/safe side, above the undercut, keeping the anti-kickback step.
  3. Cut toward the undercut to set the holding wood; sight it through the undercut. Stay off the low side.
  4. Cut toward the back and leave a backstrap: at least 10% of the tree diameter, excluding bark, using all the sapwood.
  5. Shoulder-check your escape route.
  6. Cut the backstrap at least 1" below the bored back cut, at full throttle until the tree commits.
NeverOnce the backstrap is set, never expose yourself to the back of the tree. That's where a barber chair or root pull goes.
Question 5 of 5
The tree starts to move while you're cutting the backstrap. What do you do?
Plan the exit before you need it
  • BCFSC: refresh your mental plan to move away before the final cut, keep a defensive position away from the back, be ready to move at the first sign of the tree falling.
  • When it commits, follow escape procedures, watching for root pull, barber chair, and overhead hazards.
  • Any doubt about the tree or your ability to finish the SWP: stop and get qualified help, or use another method (BCFSC lists machine assist and blasting).
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Slope-grown ponderosa pine and Douglas-fir, and fire- or beetle-killed snags, are our classic leaners. On a strata lot, the "escape route" is often a fence and a garage. If you can't clear the back, it's a rigging job, not a felling job.
