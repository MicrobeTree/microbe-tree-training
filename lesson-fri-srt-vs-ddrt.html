🧗 SRT vs DdRT — Two Climbing Systems, One Question

The industry has split on climbing systems. SRS/SRT is gaining ground for production climbing in tall trees; the moving rope system (DdRT/MRS) remains the familiar default for many climbers. This lesson breaks down the mechanics, requirements, hardware, and the judgment calls that tell you which system fits the job.

Terminology — Getting It Straight
System A

SRS — Stationary Rope System

Also called: SRT (Single Rope Technique), Fixed Rope, Static System

One end of the climbing line is fixed at the anchor point (TIP). The climber moves along the rope using ascenders, friction devices, or mechanical systems. The rope does not pass through the anchor — the anchor holds one end firm.

The "S" means the rope itself is stationary at the anchor. The climber moves independently.

System B

MRS — Moving Rope System

Also called: DdRT (Doubled Rope Technique), Double Rope, Dynamic System

The climbing line is threaded through (or over) the anchor point (TIP) so that both ends hang free. The climber ascends by taking up slack on both strands simultaneously using a friction hitch or mechanical device.

The "M" means the rope moves through the anchor as the climber goes up. The midpoint of the rope is at the anchor when fully ascended.

Why the Naming Is Confusing

"SRT" (Single Rope Technique) is a term borrowed from caving/rope access and refers to moving on a single line — it has been widely adopted in arboriculture but some use it interchangeably with "SRS." "DdRT" (Doubled rope technique) emphasizes that the rope is doubled through the redirect, not that there are two ropes. ANSI Z133-2017 uses the terms SRS (stationary rope system) and MRS (moving rope system) — use these when referencing the standard or in formal contexts.

How Each System Works — Mechanics
SRS / SRT — Rope fixed at TIP; climber moves up single line with ascender
MRS / DdRT — Rope through redirect; both strands loaded; friction hitch controls position
Comparing the Systems
SRS / SRT
✓ Advantages
  • More efficient ascending — direct 1:1 mechanical advantage
  • Less rope wear (single pass through anchor vs continuous movement through redirect)
  • Better for tall trees (50m+) — no mechanical disadvantage
  • Faster movement on long traverses
  • Rope can be pre-installed before ascent
✗ Disadvantages / Requirements
  • Requires separate descent device (rack, I'D, Rope Wrench)
  • ANSI Z133 mandates a secondary backup (Prusik or mechanical)
  • Less lateral movement flexibility during work positioning
  • Transition between ascent and descent requires deliberate system change
  • More complex for new climbers to learn
MRS / DdRT
✓ Advantages
  • Familiar to most trained arborists
  • Redirect can be repositioned without coming down
  • Fine-grained position control for multi-point pruning work
  • Descent and ascent managed through same device
  • Rope can be coiled around branches for mid-canopy anchoring
✗ Disadvantages
  • 2:1 mechanical disadvantage — harder work in tall trees
  • Increased rope wear at the redirect/ring point
  • Greater friction loss with dirty or wet rope through metal rings
  • Half the rope length available for working (both ends consumed)
  • Redirect rated WLL must account for redirected load (≈ 2× climber weight)
ANSI Z133 Requirements
Per ANSI Z133-2017 — Key Requirements for Both Systems
  • All climbing lines must have a minimum breaking strength of 22.2 kN (5,000 lbf) per ANSI Z133 Section 9.1.1
  • All hardware used as part of the climbing system must be compatible with the rope diameter and rated appropriately by the manufacturer
  • Arborists must be trained in the specific system they are using and demonstrate competency before solo use
  • Both systems require a work positioning lanyard or secondary connection when performing cutting operations — the climbing line alone is not sufficient
Specific SRS / SRT Requirements — ANSI Z133-2017 Section 9.1.5

When operating on a stationary rope system, the climber must use a secondary friction hitch or approved mechanical backup device. This is non-negotiable — SRS without a backup is non-compliant regardless of the climber's experience level.

  • The backup must be capable of arresting a fall independently if the primary system fails
  • The backup must be positioned correctly (above the primary device on ascent, below on descent — follow manufacturer guidance for your specific device)
  • Common solutions: Prusik hitch loop, Klemheist, or approved mechanical backup (e.g., Edelrid Spoc, DMM Hitch Climber with Prusik)
⚠ Redirect WLL in MRS — The Load Math Matters

In a moving rope system, the redirect point (friction saver, ring sling, or carabiner over a branch) takes approximately twice the climber's weight — both loaded strands pull through the redirect. A 90 kg climber + 15 kg gear = 105 kg. The redirect sees approximately 210 kg plus dynamic load. Ensure your friction saver, ring, or redirect hardware is rated appropriately. Swivel-type friction savers rated ≥40 kN MBS are standard industry practice.

When to Use Which System
Scenario Preferred System Reason
Tall tree (30m+) with single primary TIP SRS Mechanical efficiency; MRS ascending over 30m is exhausting and slow
Multi-point pruning work requiring lateral movement MRS Redirect flexibility; easier repositioning without system change
Long aerial traverse between anchor points SRS Single line moves with climber; MRS limits traverse to rope length/2
Shorter trees (under 15m), complex crown structure MRS Versatile redirecting in complex crown; mechanical disadvantage less significant
Production climbing — multiple similar trees SRS Speed advantage compounding across multiple setups
New or transitioning climber learning systems MRS More intuitive for most climbers; established training framework
Work positioning during chainsaw operations Both Either — but secondary lanyard required regardless of primary system; follow ANSI Z133

📋 Field Note — Okanagan Context

In the Okanagan we work on ponderosa pine and Douglas-fir that routinely exceed 30 metres — SRS is becoming the production standard for a reason. But the argument for MRS on a mature Silver maple in a Kelowna boulevard — multi-fork crown, complex canopy, lots of lateral movement — is equally valid. The best climbers in the region use both systems and switch based on the job. If you're only comfortable in one system, you're leaving efficiency and safety on the table. Take a weekend course on the system you haven't trained in formally — the ISA and TCIA offer skill development clinics for exactly this transition.
