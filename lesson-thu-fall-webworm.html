Fall Webworm (Hyphantria cunea) vs Western Tent Caterpillar

Late-summer webs on the branch tips aren't the spring tents from May. Tell them apart in five seconds, know the real risk, and give clients advice that holds up.

🕸️ Two "tent" bugs, two seasons

Same question from clients, different insect. Spring tents in the crotches are western tent caterpillar (see the Week 18 Western Tent Caterpillar lesson). Late-summer webs wrapped around the branch tips are fall webworm. Getting it right changes what you tell the client and when to act.

📍 Is fall webworm really here? Yes.

  • Native to North America, and one of the few native species accidentally introduced to Europe and Asia (NRCan).
  • In BC: records are mostly from the southern third of the province, with one northern record from Atlin (Pacific Northwest Moths).
  • In the Okanagan: listed on the Okanagan Biodiversity Checklist. The BC Ministry of Agriculture and Food's provincial insect reports said populations were "very high in the North Okanagan" in 2023, and again in 2024. In August 2023, local news (iNFOnews) reported a big webworm year in Kamloops and the Okanagan, with a photo of a web on Knox Mountain in Kelowna.

Bottom line: an established native insect with boom years, not a new invasive. The most recent provincial report we found is for 2024, so don't promise clients a bad (or quiet) year. Watch what's happening locally.

Question 1 of 5
Which statement about fall webworm in BC is accurate?
Native, established, sometimes abundant
  • NRCan: fall webworm is native to North America and was introduced from here to Europe and Asia, not the other way around.
  • Pacific Northwest Moths: BC records are mostly from the southern third of the province. In the PNW it's usually uncommon, but outbreaks are more likely in disturbed urban areas and orchards.
  • BC Ministry of Agriculture and Food reports to the Western Committee on Crop Pests: populations very high in the North Okanagan in 2023 and again in 2024.
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Urban yards, orchards, riparian willows and cottonwoods are the places to watch. When you report a web, note the host and the location. That kind of local record helps.

Question 2 of 5
Late August, West Kelowna. Loose grey webbing wraps the leaves at the ends of several chokecherry branches, and the caterpillars are inside it. What is it?
Tips + late summer + feeding inside = fall webworm
  • Fall webworm: webs on the terminal (outer tip) of branches. Wisconsin Extension says it "always places the tent on the end of the branch." Larvae feed inside the web, so it fills with droppings and leaf bits. Most noticeable mid-summer into fall.
  • Tent caterpillars usually web the inner foliage at the crotch of branches (UC IPM) and show up in spring, right after budburst (USDA FS FIDL 119).
  • Timeline (AAFC): moths emerge late June to early July, eggs go on leaf undersides, larvae feed 6–8 weeks, then drop to the litter and soil in September to pupate.

Hosts: more than 100 tree species (NRCan), including apple, chokecherry, pin cherry, aspen, balsam poplar, alder, birch, elm, ash and willow. Cottonwood, maple, mulberry, fruit and nut trees are also listed (UC IPM).

Question 3 of 5
Why is pruning out egg bands in winter a good control for western tent caterpillar but not for fall webworm?
Know where they spend the winter
  • Western tent caterpillar: eggs laid in oval masses of 150–250 that partly circle twigs, covered with frothy spumaline (FIDL 119). Snip and bag them during dormant pruning.
  • Fall webworm: overwinters as pupae in thin cocoons in bark, under leaf litter or in the soil (Wisconsin; NRCan). In spring, females lay eggs in hairy masses on the undersides of leaves.
  • So the fall webworm window is summer: find the webs while they're small.
Question 4 of 5
A strata board wants every fall webworm web on their mature willows "sprayed today." The webs are big, it's mid-September, and the trees look healthy. Best IPM advice?
Tolerate, remove, or treat early and selectively
  • Tolerance is a valid choice: Wisconsin Extension says damage is generally not significant on healthy, well-established trees. By the time leaves are being eaten, the trees have already stored enough energy, and in many cases simply tolerating the webs is acceptable.
  • Mechanical: cut out small webs if the pruning won't harm the tree's shape or health. Snag larger webs with a pole or forked stick and destroy them. UC IPM suggests twirling webs into a ball with a brush on a pole.
  • Don't burn webs. Wisconsin: burning is not needed or recommended, does more harm than the insects, and is a fire hazard. In the dry Okanagan, that's a hard no.
  • Selective products: Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) kills only butterfly and moth larvae that eat treated foliage, and spares the parasitic flies and wasps that attack webworms. UC IPM suggests a second Bt application 7–10 days later because it breaks down quickly. Sprays must be applied with enough pressure to penetrate the web (AAFC; UC IPM). Spinosad is toxic to bees, so don't use it on flowering plants (UC IPM).
  • Timing: apply while webs and caterpillars are small. Mid-September with big webs means larvae are close to dropping to pupate (AAFC), so spraying now achieves little.
📐 In BC, pesticide use is regulated under the Integrated Pest Management Act. Commercial applications need the right licence and certified applicators, and you always follow the product label.
Question 5 of 5
What's the right message to a homeowner worried their birch "is being eaten alive" by fall webworm in August?
Calm, accurate, and focused on stress
  • NRCan: even severe infestations have little impact because the damage comes near the end of the growing season, although trees may be weakened and more open to other insects and diseases.
  • Wisconsin: repeated defoliation over several years, plus other stress (drought, recent planting, construction), can weaken trees. After defoliation, make sure the tree gets enough water the next spring.
  • Natural enemies (parasitoids and predators) build up as webworms increase and usually bring populations down after a few years (Wisconsin). Spot treatments protect them.
📋 Field Note — Microbe Tree Crew

Crew standard: gloves and eye protection when pulling webs, and bag them so live larvae don't go back into the yard.
