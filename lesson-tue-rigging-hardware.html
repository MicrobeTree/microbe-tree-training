Rigging Hardware: Carabiners, Rings, Blocks & WLL vs. MBS

Most hardware failures come down to three gaps: loading it the wrong way, missing an inspection, or confusing breaking strength with working load. This card closes all three.

⏱ About 10 minutes · Week 18 · Microbe Tree Inc. crew training

Read the Numbers Right

Every rated piece of rigging hardware has its ratings stamped, printed or tagged on it. Misreading them is how good gear fails.

Two numbers, two jobs

  • MBS (minimum breaking strength): the load where the manufacturer's testing says it breaks. It's a lab number, not a working number.
  • WLL (working load limit): the most you should ever put on it in service. WLL = MBS ÷ a design factor the manufacturer chose for that product.

Design factors vary by product and standard, so never assume a ratio. If the gear only lists MBS, get the manufacturer's guidance before using it for rigging.

⚠ Why the gap mattersRigging loads are dynamic. A piece that free-falls even a short distance before the rope catches it can put several times its static weight on the block, sling and anchor. The design factor is there to absorb that, plus wear, knots and bending. It is not spare capacity to use up.
📋 Field Note — Microbe Tree Crew

Big ponderosa wood on West Kelowna lots is heavy. When you're unsure, take smaller pieces, let them run less, and keep the slack out. You can't inspect your way out of a shock load.

Carabiners: Strong One Way Only

📐 ANSI Z133-2017 §8.2.8: carabiners in a climber's work-positioning (suspension) system shall be self-closing and self-double-locking, need at least two consecutive, deliberate actions to unlock, and must withstand 5,000 lbf (22.24 kN) along the major axis with the gate closed.

Three ratings on the spine

  • Major axis, gate closed: the big number. This is the only direction a carabiner is designed to be loaded.
  • Minor axis (cross-loaded): a fraction of the major-axis rating.
  • Gate open: also a fraction. An open or not-fully-closed gate cuts strength by roughly two-thirds (TCI Magazine).

Check the actual numbers on your carabiners. They differ by model.

Loading rules

  • Load the spine, not the gate. Watch for three-way (tri-axial) loading and ropes or slings levering over the nose.
  • Shapes: D / offset-D keep the load on the spine; HMS / pear has room for a Munter hitch; ovals are symmetrical but weaker than a D of the same size.
  • Keep life-support and rigging hardware separate. Gear that has taken rigging shock loads doesn't go back into a climbing system.

Blocks, Rings & Pulleys

What each does

  • Rigging block: a sheave between side plates, hung with a sling. It redirects the lowering line with low friction and is rated for rigging loads.
  • Rigging ring / friction ring: a smooth, low-friction redirect with no moving parts. Common for light to moderate rigging and on adjustable friction-saver setups.
  • Pulley: a sheave for redirects and mechanical-advantage systems. Climbing pulleys and rigging pulleys are rated differently, so use the right one.

Sheave size and rope fit

The tighter a rope bends, the more strength it loses. Larger sheaves are kinder to rope. Always match the rope diameter to the range the manufacturer specifies for that block or pulley; too big a rope will jam or crush.

Anchor load is more than the piece

A block redirecting a lowering line carries the load on both legs of the rope. When the legs run nearly parallel, the block and its anchor see close to double the line tension, and more again under shock. Pick an anchor limb with that in mind.

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Dead and drought-stressed limbs are common on our dry sites. A limb that looks strong enough for the block may not be strong enough for twice the load plus a jolt. When in doubt, go lower or closer to the stem.

Inspect Before Every Job

Retire or quarantine if you see

  • Cracks, deep grooves, sharp edges or burrs (rope-worn grooves can cut a line)
  • Bent or distorted bodies, gates or side plates
  • Gates or locks that don't snap fully closed and lock on their own
  • Sheaves that wobble, bind or don't spin freely
  • Heavy corrosion, heat damage, or markings you can no longer read
  • Any piece that's been dropped from height onto a hard surface or has taken a severe shock load. Follow the manufacturer's guidance
📐 Follow the manufacturer's instructions and the inspection requirements of ANSI Z133 and the WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation. Tag defective gear out of service right away so nobody grabs it off the truck.
📋 Field Note — Microbe Tree Crew

Dust and grit from dry Okanagan sites wear sheaves and gate hinges. Wipe gear down, and if a lock gets sticky, clean and lubricate it per the manufacturer. Never force it.

Tailgate Quick Check

  1. What's the WLL of each piece in today's rigging system? Read it off the gear and say it out loud.
  2. Is every carabiner loaded on its major axis, gate closed and locked?
  3. Does the block's rope range match our lowering line?
  4. Is the anchor limb good for roughly double the line tension, plus shock?
  5. Any gear tagged out? Is it off the truck?

Exam angle

Expect ISA questions on carabiner requirements (self-closing, self-double-locking, 5,000 lbf / 22.24 kN), keeping climbing and rigging equipment separate, and what happens to forces at a redirect.
