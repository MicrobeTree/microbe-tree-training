Trucker's Hitch

A compound knot system that creates mechanical advantage — and how to use it correctly in arborist work

What It Is

The Trucker's Hitch

The trucker's hitch is not a single knot — it's a knot system: an anchor point, a pulley loop in the standing line, and a securing hitch at the load end. Together, they create a 3:1 mechanical advantage for tensioning a line.

Think of it as a field-improvised block-and-tackle using just rope and knowing where to put a loop.

  • Creates 3:1 mechanical advantage (ideally)
  • Jam-proof release — one pull on the slip loop and it comes apart
  • The securing hitch (two half hitches) holds tension after hauling
  • Common variants use a slip loop, a directional figure-8, or an alpine butterfly as the pulley loop

Quick Stats

Load & Efficiency

Mechanical advantage of 3:1 means a 100 kg load requires ~33 kg of pull to lift (ignoring friction). Friction loss is real — actual MA closer to 2.5:1 in practice.

  • MA (theoretical): 3:1
  • MA (practical): ~2.5:1 due to friction
  • Knot efficiency: Not rated for suspended arborist loads
  • Primary applications: Load securing, tensioning tag lines, lashing
  • NOT for: Dynamic loads, primary rigging with workers below
  • Securing hitch: Two half hitches on the standing line
  • WorkSafeBC: Load securing requirements under OHSR Part 16

🪢 How to Tie It — Step by Step
1

Secure to the anchor point

Pass the rope around your anchor (truck stake, tree, rigging point). Tie a bowline or two half hitches at the anchor — something that won't slip under load. The running end now extends out toward your load.

For load securing on trucks: wrap over and under the stake pocket or rail, then proceed.

2

Form the pulley loop in the standing line

Roughly midway between anchor and load, form a slip loop (a bight pulled through itself), a directional figure-8 on a bight, or an alpine butterfly. The loop must be able to bear a load without closing — an alpine butterfly is the most secure option; a simple slip loop is fastest but can tighten under sustained load.

The alpine butterfly is preferred when repeated use or high tension is expected — it doesn't jam and can be untied after loading.

3

Pass the running end through/around the load and back through the loop

Run the rope to your load point (hook, tie-down, cargo), around it, and return the running end through your pulley loop. Now you have a 2-part line on the load side of the loop — this is where the mechanical advantage comes from.

4

Haul the running end to tension the system

Pull the running end away from the loop (toward the anchor side of the system). The loop acts as a pulley — your 1-part pull generates tension across a 2-part load side, giving approximately 3:1 advantage (counting the anchor line as part 3). Haul until load is secure and tight.

5

Lock off with two half hitches on the standing line

While maintaining tension, pass the running end around the standing line and tie two half hitches (a clove hitch around the standing line is also acceptable). This holds the tension without you needing to maintain hand pressure. The slip loop means the whole system releases with one pull on the loop tail.

Check: the tail of the two half hitches should point away from load direction and be long enough to grip reliably — minimum 15 cm tail per standard load-securing practice.

⚙️ Applications & Limitations

When to Use It

  • Securing brush loads, logs, and equipment on trailers and truck beds
  • Tensioning tag lines to control direction of a falling piece (static load)
  • Lashing equipment to anchors (ground stakes, vehicle tie-downs)
  • Tightening a rope guide wire on a small lift where the load is static
  • Anywhere you need tension without mechanical hardware and you have enough rope

When NOT to Use It

  • NOT for primary rigging with a climber or a worker below the load — no rated WLL, dynamic load capacity unverified
  • NOT as a substitute for rated rigging hardware (pulleys, slings, shackles) in critical lift scenarios
  • NOT for long-duration holds where a slip loop is used — the slip loop can migrate under sustained load; use alpine butterfly instead
  • NOT for synthetic rope under severe heat or UV exposure — inspect rope condition first
✅ Do & Don't
✓ Do

Use an alpine butterfly or directional figure-8 for the pulley loop in arborist applications — they're load-rated and won't jam. Inspect rope before using as a load-securing system. Pad rope over sharp edges (truck stake corners). Check tension mid-trip on long hauls — rope stretches, wood settles.

✗ Don't

Don't use a simple overhand slip loop as the pulley when loads are heavy or sustained — it can tighten and become impossible to release. Don't use a trucker's hitch as a primary life-safety rigging component. Don't confuse the 3:1 MA with a rope's WLL — the two are unrelated. The MA tells you how much easier it is to pull; the WLL tells you how much load the rope can bear.

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

In the field, you'll use the trucker's hitch most often for securing brush loads on the trailer between dump runs, and for tensioning a control line when you need directional influence on a limb without adding rope-to-rope mechanical friction from a block. It's a foundational competency — fast to tie, fast to release, and it works with whatever rope you have in your hand. Per WorkSafeBC OHSR Part 16.32, all loads transported on a vehicle must be secured so they cannot shift, fall, or be blown from the vehicle. A properly tensioned trucker's hitch meets this requirement when applied correctly. For trailer loads exceeding what rope tension can secure, rated tie-down straps with J-hooks and ratchets are the right tool — the trucker's hitch is supplemental for oddly shaped or loose material that needs additional binding. ISA ANSI Z133 Section 8 covers equipment transport — know the standard.
