Douglas-fir Tussock Moth: Winter Egg Masses & Tussockosis

How to spot the egg masses on a winter job, why the hairs matter for the crew, and what BC says about populations right now.

🐛 Douglas-fir tussock moth (Orgyia pseudotsugata)

  • A native defoliator of the low-lying, dry-belt Douglas-fir regions of southern BC (BC info sheet). It occurs only in low-elevation stands (BC Ministry of Forests).
  • Hosts: mainly Douglas-fir, occasionally ponderosa pine and western larch. In urban areas, ornamental spruce and pine can also be hit.
  • Damage: new needles go first and the tops of crowns and branch tips turn reddish-brown. Severe outbreaks can defoliate and kill trees. NRCan notes it can be confused with western spruce budworm, but budworm feeds mainly on new growth while tussock moth eats new and old needles.
  • Outbreak cycle: the BC Forest Health Bulletin (Jan 2026) says outbreaks come about every 7–12 years and last up to four years before natural controls (mainly a virus) collapse them.

❄️ Winter ID: the egg mass

  • It overwinters as eggs. There's one generation a year, and eggs hatch in spring (BC bulletin; NRCan).
  • The female is wingless and stays on her cocoon. She lays all her eggs (about 200 in BC) in one mass on her empty cocoon (NRCan).
  • What it looks like: NRCan says egg masses are about 13 mm across, made of round white eggs in one to three layers, covered with a frothy secretion mixed with body hairs that hardens. The cocoon underneath is grey-brown, about 20 mm, made of silk and hairs.
  • Where: cocoons are spun on foliage and trunks and on picnic tables and other objects (BC bulletin). In 2025, egg masses were found on trees, lawn furniture and other yard objects in Kamloops subdivisions.
  • Females don't fly, so infestations are patchy. Young larvae spread by ballooning on silk, sometimes for several hundred metres (NRCan).
⚠️ Crew safety: tussockosisBC's info sheet says the caterpillars carry thousands of tiny hairs, and hairs are also on the female moth, egg masses and cocoons. They can cause an allergic reaction called tussockosis: mostly itching, but also rashes, watery eyes and sneezing. Nearly 1 in 5 people get symptoms. In very sensitive people, reactions can build with repeated exposure. The Jan 2026 bulletin adds that urticating hairs can be released into the air, and severe cases can include anaphylaxis.

🧤 What we do (from the BC info sheet)

  • Don't handle any life stage barehanded. Wear gloves. Keep your safety glasses on when you're working through infested foliage.
  • Wash your hands and exposed skin often with soap and water. Change clothes after heavy-infestation work, and don't shake your clothes out before washing them.
  • Wash equipment before you use it again (rope bags and tarps too).
  • Severe symptoms: get medical attention immediately. Tell your lead if anyone on the crew has a known allergy history, because BC says those people may be more susceptible.

📊 Current status (BC government, latest published)

  • BC's 2025 Forest Health Conditions report for the Southern Interior (Dec 17, 2025) says no Douglas-fir tussock moth defoliation was mapped in 2025. There were numerous single-tree outbreaks in the Kamloops and Okanagan areas.
  • Of 37 trap sites in the Thompson Okanagan Region, 10 averaged over 25 moths per trap, the first year since 2020 that so many did. The Okanagan outbreak region had 40% of its sites over 25.
  • BC's outlook: there may be localized defoliation in urban-interface areas in 2026, and an outbreak in surrounding forests may occur within the next few years. BC's rule of thumb is that sites over 25 moths per trap for 2–3 consecutive years mean an outbreak is likely imminent.
📋 Field Note: winter pruning on Douglas-fir

You're limbing up a yard Douglas-fir and find frothy, hairy, greyish-white lumps on old cocoons under a branch and on the client's deck railing. Stop handling them barehanded, glove up, and take a photo. Tell Anton. Any control or treatment decision is his call, and BC asks people to report new infestations to the Ministry of Forests regional office in Kamloops.
