Cold Acclimation & Dormancy: How Trees Get Ready for Winter

Dormancy isn't just "winter sleep." It's a step-by-step program that starts with day length, gets finished by frost, and can be thrown off by what we do on site.

⏱ About 10 minutes · Week 18 · Microbe Tree Inc. crew training

🌿 The short version

Trees don't wait for the first frost to get ready for winter. Shortening days stop growth and start the hardening process; frost and cold then finish it. Get between a tree and that schedule (late fertilizer, late pruning, a warm October irrigation push) and you can leave it soft when the first Arctic outflow pours down the valley.

Short days → growth stops, hardening begins
Frost / low temps → full hardiness
Won't grow even if warm — needs chilling
Ready to grow — held back by cold
Question 1 of 5
In the classic two-stage model of cold acclimation in woody plants, what triggers the first stage?
Day length starts it, frost finishes it

Work on apple and red-osier dogwood (Howell & Weiser 1970; Weiser 1970) showed acclimation runs in two stages. Stage 1 is induced by short days: leaves sense the longer nights (through phytochrome), shoot growth stops, and a signal from the leaves starts the hardening process in the bark. Stage 2 is induced by frost or sustained low temperature and takes the tissue to maximum hardiness.

  • In Howell & Weiser's apple study, stage 1 hardened bark to about −30 °C; frost was needed to reach about −55 °C.
  • The key event in stage 1 is growth cessation. A tree that is still pushing shoots isn't hardening.
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Our autumns can be warm and dry right up until an Arctic outflow drops temperatures fast. Trees that stopped growing on schedule handle it. Trees kept growing by late irrigation, nitrogen or heavy summer pruning are the ones that show dieback in May.

Question 2 of 5
A strata client wants a heavy nitrogen fertilizer application and a big thinning prune in late August to "get the trees ready for winter." What's the best advice?
Don't wake the tree up when it's trying to shut down

Anything that pushes new, succulent growth late in the season works against hardening. Late nitrogen and heavy pruning can both trigger a late flush of shoots that are still soft when the first hard freeze arrives. The result is tip dieback, cambium injury and an easy entry point for cankers like Cytospora.

  • Fertilize (if a soil or foliar test says it's needed) in spring or early summer.
  • Heavy structural pruning is best in the dormant season, or after the spring flush has hardened.
  • Taper irrigation in early fall so trees can slow down, but don't send them into winter bone-dry. A deep watering before freeze-up helps evergreens.
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Strata irrigation often runs on the same summer schedule into October. Flag it on fall visits. Tapering water in September is one of the cheapest winter-injury fixes we can recommend.

Question 3 of 5
A tree in endodormancy (sometimes called "rest") is moved into a warm greenhouse in November. What happens?
Endo vs. eco: who's holding the brakes?

Dormancy researchers (Lang et al. 1987) split dormancy by where the block is:

  • Endodormancy: the block is inside the bud. Even warm, perfect conditions won't make it grow. It's released by accumulating chilling (hours at cool, above-freezing temperatures). The amount needed varies by species and cultivar.
  • Ecodormancy: chilling is satisfied and the bud is ready, but the environment (cold) holds it back. Warmth now leads to budbreak.
  • Paradormancy: growth held back by another part of the plant, like apical dominance from the leader.

That's why a midwinter warm spell doesn't usually trigger budbreak in December, but the same warm spell in late February or March can.

Question 4 of 5
In late winter, a week of +12 °C weather is followed by a sudden drop to −15 °C. Why is this more dangerous than a steady −15 °C in January?
Deacclimation is fast; re-hardening is slow

Once chilling is satisfied (ecodormancy), warm spells cause deacclimation: tissues lose cold hardiness, sometimes within days. Re-hardening after a warm spell is slower and often incomplete. A sharp freeze right after a warm spell is a classic setup for:

  • Bud and twig kill, especially on early-moving species and fruit trees
  • Southwest winter injury / sunscald: sun-warmed bark on the south and southwest side, then a rapid temperature drop at sunset
  • Frost cracks: vertical splits from uneven contraction of the stem
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Thin-barked young trees on south- and west-facing strata slopes take the worst of it. When you're diagnosing a spring canker or a vertical bark split, ask what the trunk faces and check for sunscald history before blaming a pest.

Question 5 of 5
Which plant hormone is most closely associated with promoting bud dormancy and stress responses as trees shut down for winter?
ABA: the shut-down signal

Abscisic acid (ABA) rises in many temperate trees as they move into dormancy and cold acclimation. It's linked to bud set, growth cessation and stress tolerance, and it's the same hormone that closes stomata in drought. Gibberellins generally work the other way, promoting growth and budbreak, and the balance between them shifts as chilling accumulates.

For the ISA exam, remember the families: auxin (apical dominance, rooting, wound response), cytokinin (cell division, made in roots), gibberellin (shoot elongation, breaking dormancy), ABA (dormancy, stomatal closure), ethylene (ripening, abscission, stress).

📋 Field Note — Crew

Dormant-season pruning works well partly because trees are shut down: no active leaf area to lose, structure easy to see, and for many species lower pest pressure. Just confirm species-specific timing first (fire blight hosts, oaks and elms have their own rules).
