Cold Hardiness & Winter Injury: How Trees Harden Off

Frost cracks and sunscald aren't random. Know how trees harden off, and where the Okanagan's sun-then-freeze swings break that, and you'll spot the risk before the crack.

🥶 The short answer

Trees don't survive winter by staying warm. They harden off: short autumn days start the change, then colder temperatures finish it. The injuries we see on the job (frost cracks, sunscald) come when that hardiness is lost or never finished: a late growth flush, a drought going into winter, or a sunny warm-up followed by a hard freeze. The Okanagan's dry-interior winter, with sun by day and a hard freeze by night, is exactly that recipe.

Shorter days: partial hardiness
Subfreezing temps: maximum mid-winter hardiness
Vertical split in bark and wood, S/SW side of young trees
Dead bark and cambium on the S/SW side after a warm day then a freeze

What happens inside the cells

  • In a hardy tree, ice forms between the cells, not inside them. Water leaves the cells and they shrink. Hardy cells tolerate that dehydration (WSU FS196E).
  • Hardening brings more sugar in the cells, protein changes and a more tolerant plasma membrane (WSU FS196E).
  • If the cold hits suddenly, water may not get out in time and ice forms inside the cells, which can burst them (WSU FS196E).
  • Not all tissues harden equally: stems are hardier than vegetative buds, which are hardier than flower buds. Roots are among the least hardy, but soil insulates them (WSU FS196E).

Sunscald vs frost crack

  • Sunscald (southwest injury): winter sun warms the S/SW bark, dormant cells wake up and lose hardiness, then a freeze kills the phloem and cambium just under the bark. Look for discoloured, cracked, sunken or sloughing bark (USU).
  • Frost crack: a vertical split in bark and wood, usually on the S or SW side of young, thin-barked trees after severe cold. Experts don't all agree on the exact cause, but all link it to temperature swings in the trunk. One explanation is uneven shrinking and swelling of the wood as water leaves the cell walls to freeze. Old wounds and branch stubs are often the starting point (WSU FS199E).
⚠ Don'tDon't paint or seal a frost crack or sunscald wound. The wood never truly seals: the crack stays and invites decay fungi. Wound dressings don't prevent decay (WSU FS199E, USU).
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

USU says Utah is prone to sunscald because of cold winters and little cloud on sunny winter days. Our dry interior has the same setup: clear, sunny days that warm south-facing bark, then freezing nights. Newly planted maples, lindens, cherries and other thin-barked trees on a south or west exposure are the ones to check on any winter walk-around. If it's cracked, flag it as a defect and look for decay.

🧠 Memory hook: "Short days start it, cold finishes it, a warm spell undoes it."
Question 1 of 5
A fall visit: the customer fertilized and heavily pruned their young maple in late summer, and it flushed new growth. Why does this raise winter-injury risk?
Late growth delays hardening

WSU Extension lists late-summer to early-fall fertilizing and pruning as causes of cold injury: they stimulate growth, and actively growing plants cannot acclimate. Delayed dormancy means delayed hardening (WSU FS196E).

  • Stage 1 of hardening is triggered by shortening days. Stage 2 needs subfreezing temperatures.
  • Drought stress going into winter also raises risk, so water well before the ground freezes.
Question 2 of 5
Which sequence describes sunscald (southwest injury) on a young, thin-barked tree?
Warm day, hard freeze

Sunscald injures the living cells just inside the bark (phloem and cambium) through day-to-night temperature swings. Warmed cells become active and lose some cold hardiness, then freeze when the temperature drops (USU).

  • USU reports the south side of a tree can be as much as 77°F warmer than the north side on a cold winter day.
  • Signs: discoloured or cracked bark, sunken areas, sloughing bark that exposes dead tissue.
  • Drought-stressed trees are more susceptible.
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

On a clear winter day, look at which side of the trunk the damage sits on. S/SW bark, young tree, thin bark: think sunscald before you think disease.

Question 3 of 5
You find a long vertical split down the southwest side of a young linden in February. What is the best advice for the customer?
Don't seal frost cracks

WSU Extension: callus may close over the edge, but the wood never seals. Once fibres are split the crack remains and is an entry point for decay fungi and insects. Wound dressings are ineffective at preventing decay, so don't use them.

  • Best treatment: maintain tree vigour with appropriate irrigation, fertilization, mulching and pest management.
  • A cracked tree may live many useful years, but watch for extensive rot. WSU suggests consulting a TRAQ arborist if it may be hazardous.
  • Prevention: protect the S/SW trunk from warming on cold sunny days (white or light-coloured guards applied in late fall and removed in spring), and avoid trunk wounds.
Question 4 of 5
Which statement about cold hardiness is correct (ISA exam favourite)?
Tissues differ, and so do triggers
  • Order of hardiness: stems > vegetative buds > flower buds > roots (young roots are less hardy than older roots) (WSU FS196E).
  • Triggers: shortening day length (photoperiod) starts acclimation, then low temperatures bring maximum hardiness. Deacclimation depends mainly on temperature, so a winter warm spell can strip hardiness and cold returning can injure the plant.
  • Hardiness is genetically determined, but environment and cultural practices influence it. Plants from different origins respond to day length differently.
📋 ISA exam

Tree Biology is 11% of the exam. Know the 3 parts of hardiness: acclimation, maximum mid-winter hardiness, deacclimation.

Question 5 of 5
You're doing fall planting and wrap-up on a job with a thin-barked young tree on the south side of a house. Which plan best prevents sunscald and frost cracks?
Hydrate, reflect, remove
  • Hydrate: trees that go into winter well watered are less prone to sunscald (USU).
  • Reflect or shade: white tree wrap or a light-coloured board/fence on the S and W keeps dormant cells cool and inactive (USU, WSU).
  • Timing: apply in late fall, remove in spring once freezing nights are past. Wrap left on may harbour pests and can constrict trunk growth.
  • Site: plant susceptible trees where evergreens or buildings shade the lower trunk in winter.
📋 Field Note — Microbe Tree Crew

Put a wrap-removal reminder on the job ticket. Tree guards that get forgotten in spring become the next problem.
