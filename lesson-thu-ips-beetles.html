Ips pini (the pine engraver beetle) is the primary Ips species attacking pines in the BC Interior, including the Okanagan. It attacks ponderosa pine, lodgepole pine, and to a lesser extent other pine species. It is well-documented throughout the Thompson-Okanagan and is the species most arborists encounter on urban and interface pines.

Identification summary — Ips pini:

  • Adult: 3–5mm, reddish-brown to black; hind end (elytra) has a distinctly concave depression with 4 pairs of spines — the spine count is diagnostic (Ips species are distinguished by spine count)
  • Attacks mid-trunk to upper trunk and large branches

  • Males initiate attack, excavate nuptial chamber, and release pheromones (ipsdienol, cis-verbenol) to attract females

Gallery patterns are the most reliable field diagnostic for distinguishing bark beetle species. They are engraved in the phloem-cambium interface and remain readable on dead wood long after the beetles have emerged.

Ips gallery pattern: A central nuptial chamber (the mating and egg-laying room, roughly round or oval) with 2–6 egg galleries radiating outward from the chamber — the pattern looks like a capital I, H, Y, or asterisk depending on how many females the male mates with. Egg niches are small chambers off the galleries where individual eggs are laid. Larval galleries then radiate from the egg niches, widening as larvae grow, creating a "herringbone" appearance around each egg gallery.

Mountain pine beetle (Dendroctonus ponderosae) gallery pattern: Vertical, winding, primarily vertical egg galleries — often described as a "vertical S" or irregular winding gallery running with the grain. MPB is a monogamous system (one male, one female per gallery system); the gallery is less branched than Ips.

Other species: Spruce beetle (D. rufipennis) makes similar vertical galleries in spruce. Western pine beetle (D. brevicomis) makes winding irregular galleries in ponderosa pine — often confused with MPB but less common in the Okanagan.

Ips flight distance is not the limiting factor — adults can fly several kilometres under outbreak conditions, and even endemic populations can find stressed trees at significant distances via host volatile cues. Ips species are attracted to the terpene volatiles produced by damaged or stressed trees, which they can detect at very low concentrations.

The central variable is host tree vigour and resin flow capacity. A healthy, well-watered ponderosa pine in deep loam soil with adequate root zone can produce enough resin flow to pitch out attacking beetles — even under moderate attack pressure. This is a well-documented phenomenon called constitutive resin defense. Stressed trees, by contrast, have reduced resin duct development, lower resin production rates, and reduced monoterpene concentrations — all of which compromise their ability to resist attack.

The correct advice to the client:

  • Assess the vigour of their trees: Is the crown full and green? Any crown thinning or dieback? Any history of root disturbance or drought?
  • Ensure the removed tree's slash (cut logs, bark) is removed or treated promptly — slash is a brood emergence site and can produce thousands of beetles in the following season
  • Begin or increase irrigation if trees are moisture-stressed — this is the single most effective preventive measure
  • Monitor for frass and pitch tubes monthly through the growing season
  • Preventive bark spray (carbaryl, bifenthrin) can be applied to the trunk and large branches of high-value at-risk trees — effectiveness is best when applied before beetle flight in spring

Ips is not restricted to ponderosa pine — I. pini also attacks lodgepole. Flight timing spans multiple emergence windows across the season in BC (not limited to 2 weeks). The "30 metre" limit is false.

Blue stain is one of the most visually striking field signs in bark beetle-killed wood — a blue-grey to dark blue-black discolouration of the sapwood, often with a marbling pattern. It is caused by ophiostomatoid fungi (Ophiostoma and Grosmannia species), which are vectored by bark beetles in specialized structures called mycangia (spore-carrying pouches on the beetle's body).

The relationship is mutualistic: The fungi break down wood cells and create conditions favourable for larval development; the beetles provide transport and dispersal. The fungi do not attack the beetle.

Arboricultural significance:

  • Blue stain fungi colonize the sapwood's tracheids and block water transport pathways — accelerating tree death alongside the direct phloem destruction by beetle galleries
  • Blue stain is a reliable indicator that bark beetle activity was involved in tree decline or death — distinguishes it from drought death alone
  • Blue-stained wood retains structural integrity — the fungi consume only cell contents, not cell walls. Blue-stained lumber is structurally sound (it's a cosmetic defect, not a structural one) — relevant if a client asks about using the timber
  • Blue stain fungi are not transmissible to adjacent healthy trees by contact — they require beetle vector to spread
