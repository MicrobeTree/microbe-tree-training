Leaf Abscission & Fall Colour: How Trees Let Go

Every October the phone rings: "Why is my tree turning early?" "Should I prune now?" "Why won't the leaves fall?" Here's the biology behind the answers.

🍂 The short version

A leaf doesn't just fall off. The tree cuts it loose on purpose. Shorter days and cooler nights start the shutdown. The tree pulls nutrients back out of the leaf, builds a weak "tear here" line at the base of the leaf stalk, and seals the wound as the leaf comes away. The colours are a side effect of that shutdown.

Shorter days + cooler temps start senescence

Ethylene → enzymes dissolve cell glue
Carotenoids, there all summer
Anthocyanins, made new in fall

✂️ What it means for pruning

  • Iowa State Extension: late dormant season is generally the best time to prune most deciduous trees. Where you can, avoid pruning while trees are leafing out in spring or dropping leaves in fall.
  • Dead, broken or hazardous wood (storm damage, for example) can come out at any time of year.
  • With the leaves off, you get a clear view of the structure, which is one reason Iowa State favours the late dormant season.
📋 Field Note — Microbe Tree Crew

October calls for "a quick trim before the leaves are gone" are common. Hazard and clearance work, yes. Routine structural pruning on deciduous trees: book it for the dormant season, once the leaves are down.

Question 1 of 5
What actually happens in the abscission zone before a leaf drops?
A planned cut, sealed before it's finished

The abscission zone is a narrow band of cells at the base of the leaf stalk (petiole). When it activates:

  • Cells release pectinases and cellulases that dissolve the middle lamella, the pectin-rich layer cementing neighbouring cells together.
  • Once those cells separate, the leaf is held on only by its vascular strands. Wind finishes the job.
  • On the tree side, a protective layer forms at the same time or just after. On the leaf scar it's one or more suberized (corky) cell layers that cut water loss and keep pathogens out.

The same process also lets trees drop leaves early under stress (drought, pathogens, wounding), sometimes without the usual senescence.

📋 Field Note — ISA exam

Know the words: senescence (the planned shutdown), abscission (the separation), leaf scar (the sealed wound left on the twig). Leaf scars are also a winter ID feature.

Question 2 of 5
Which hormone change sets abscission off?
Less auxin, more ethylene

Classic experiments (Addicott and others, reviewed by Jin, SLU 2015) showed that a healthy leaf sends auxin down the petiole, and that keeps the abscission zone switched off. Put auxin on a cut petiole and abscission is delayed.

  • As the leaf senesces, auxin from the leaf side falls. Ethylene production and/or sensitivity in the zone rises, and that triggers the hydrolytic enzymes.
  • Wisconsin Extension's version: ethylene builds up at the base of the leaf stalk and breaks down the cells that hold the leaf on.
  • Auxin, gibberellins and brassinosteroids generally hold off abscission. Ethylene, ABA, jasmonic acid and cytokinins have been shown to speed it up.

Last week's dormancy lesson had ABA as the "shut-down" hormone in buds. This week, ethylene is the one that actually drops the leaf.

Question 3 of 5
Why do some trees turn yellow and others turn red?
Unmasked vs. newly made
  • Carotenoids (yellow, orange) work alongside chlorophyll all season. As chlorophyll is broken down in fall, they show through.
  • Anthocyanins (reds, purples) are made in autumn, from sugars trapped in the leaf. Wisconsin Extension's research summary says they shade the leaf's machinery from bright light while the tree is still recovering nutrients. Red leaves on the sunny side of a crown are often the brightest.
  • Tannins give the browns and coppers (oaks, for example) once the other pigments fade.

Weather: cool, sunny days give the brightest colour. Very warm autumns shorten the show and reduce reds. A hard freeze can kill leaves before the best colour develops.

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Our sunny fall days and cool nights fit that "cool and sunny" pattern, but a warm October or an early hard freeze can change a season's colour. Up in the hills, the gold in the conifer stands is western larch, a native deciduous conifer whose needles turn yellow in autumn and drop for winter. Good exam trivia, and clients ask about it.

Question 4 of 5
Mid-August, a strata client says one boulevard maple is "already turning" while the rest of the row is green. What's the best response?
Early colour = possible cry for help

Purdue Extension says individual trees that colour well ahead of others of the same species may be "shouting out a proverbial cry for help." Their check list:

  • Is there a visible root flare, or was it planted deep or buried by a grade change?
  • Any trunk, root flare or surface-root damage (possible hidden decay)?
  • Girdling roots?
  • Mulched, or competing with turf? Signs of earlier drought?
  • Compaction, or construction within the last 5 years?

Wisconsin Extension adds that trees next to streets, driveways and buildings often colour early from heat off pavement, compacted soil and de-icing salts. Above-ground symptoms can lag root injury by years.

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Hot, dry summers and narrow boulevard strips are a perfect setup for this. When a client asks "is it dying?", don't guess. Explain that early colour is a symptom, not a diagnosis, and offer an assessment.

Question 5 of 5
After an early hard freeze, a client's tree is still holding a full set of brown, dead leaves in November. What's going on, and what's the risk?
Marcescence: the cut never finished
  • K-State Extension: early freezes can kill still-green leaves and interrupt the normal shutdown, so the abscission layer never completes. That's marcescence ("withering without falling off"). Trees showing it are not necessarily harmed. Watch the next spring for real damage.
  • Some trees (oaks, beeches) hold dead leaves normally. UNH Extension notes that a long, mild fall can also leave late-colouring trees like Japanese maple with incomplete abscission layers.
  • Risk: a tree without leaves is much less likely to break under ice or heavy wet snow. If that snow arrives while leaves are still hanging, expect more limb failures.
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

An early wet snow on trees still in leaf is a busy day for storm calls. Separately: on apples, crabapples, pears, hawthorns and mountain-ash, our fire blight timing lesson notes that blighted branches often hold dead leaves into winter. Dead leaves on one or two branches of a bare tree are worth a closer look.
