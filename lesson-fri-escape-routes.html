Escape Routes, Retreat Paths
& Felling Strategies

Last week covered notch geometry. This week covers what happens before the notch — and immediately after the tree starts moving. The saw work is only one part of felling. The other part is knowing where your feet go when it all goes according to plan, and where they go when it doesn't.

⬡ Escape Route Fundamentals

📜 ANSI Z133 — Section 8.1.1

"Before starting a felling operation, each worker shall designate an escape route and clear it of obstructions. The escape route shall be established to move away from the expected felling direction."

The escape route is not an afterthought — it is the first thing you plan at any felling site. Before the saw comes off the truck. Before you assess the lean. Before you do anything else. ANSI Z133 mandates it, and incident analysis consistently shows that failure to pre-plan escape routes is a contributing factor in most felling fatalities.

Escape Route Geometry

Escape routes run at approximately 45° to the LEFT and RIGHT of the back of the intended fall direction. Never retreat directly away from the stump along the fall path — the butt kick zone and rolling butt are there.

The 45° angle is the key. Retreat paths that run directly away from the fall direction put you in the path of:

  • Butt kick: As the tree falls forward, the base of the trunk can leap backward with significant force — this is called the "barber chair" component when combined with upward trunk splits
  • Rolling stump: On slopes, stumps can roll unpredictably after separation
  • Root plate: Root plate can lift as the tree falls, then slam back down

The 45° angle takes you off all these hazard paths while still giving you maximum distance from the falling tree in the shortest time.

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

On Okanagan hillside properties — and we work on a lot of them — the slope changes your escape route calculation. Retreating uphill is generally better than downhill (better footing, less chance of tripping on debris). But check the route: on steep terrain with loose rock or steep grade changes, a "clear" route on paper can be a roll hazard in practice. Walk both routes before starting. Note where the footing is solid. Note obstacles: irrigation heads, deck steps, rocks, garden beds. Pre-plan while your head is clear.

⬡ Clearing the Escape Route

An escape route that hasn't been cleared is a trap. Per ANSI Z133 8.1.1: the route must be cleared of obstructions before work begins. In practice:

  • Remove loose debris (cut rounds, limbs, tools) from both routes — 10 m minimum clear distance
  • Identify and mark trip hazards you can't move (stumps, irrigation, cables)
  • Ensure footing is solid — in wet conditions or on frost, soft ground changes traction significantly
  • Communicate route locations to all crew members at tailgate — everyone knows where everyone else is going
📜 ANSI Z133 — Exclusion Zone

All personnel not directly involved in the felling operation must be clear of a zone equal to two tree lengths in all directions from the tree before cutting begins. This is the minimum exclusion zone. One worker must be designated as spotter/lookout during felling.

⬡ Pre-Felling Size-Up Sequence

The felling sequence begins long before the first cut. This is the complete pre-felling assessment per ANSI Z133 and TCIA best practices:

Assess lean and weight distribution

Stand back at least one tree length. Assess lean in two axes — front/back AND left/right. Use a plumb line or sighting stick if lean is ambiguous. Identify the direction of natural fall (lean almost always wins).

Check for defects and hazards

Scan for: dead tops, widow makers, hangers from previous work, cracks in the stem, cavities, decay signs, loose bark. Check the root collar for root plate integrity. Note any hazards that will affect felling direction or increase risk.

Identify target zone and confirm felling direction

Confirm what is in the target zone — structures, utilities, other trees, crew members. Confirm the intended felling direction aligns with lean and is achievable with the notch design. If lean conflicts with safe felling direction, assess whether rigging, wedges, or a different strategy is required.

Designate and clear escape routes

Walk both escape routes. Remove obstructions. Brief crew. Confirm everyone knows the designated escape routes before any cutting begins.

Position all personnel and issue clear signal

Confirm two-tree-length exclusion zone is clear. Designate a spotter. Establish clear communication signals before starting the saw (verbal or hand signal protocol). ANSI Z133 8.1.3: "all persons shall be positioned where they can observe the tree and be out of the felling zone."

Plan the cut sequence

Decide on notch type (open face recommended per modern practice), notch depth (70–80% of trunk diameter for open face), and backcut position. Determine hinge wood target: approximately 10% of trunk diameter. Have wedges and a second saw accessible if needed.

⬡ Felling Execution: Three-Phase Sequence

Phase 1 — The Notch

  • Make the top cut first (open face: 70° opening angle, 70–80% diameter depth)
  • Bottom cut meets top cut at the notch apex
  • Remove the face-piece cleanly — don't leave a shelf
  • Notch faces the intended fall direction exactly

Phase 2 — The Backcut

  • Backcut is level (horizontal) and positioned above the notch apex by 5–8 cm (open face advantage)
  • Leave the hinge intact: do NOT cut to the notch
  • Hinge width: approximately 80% of diameter
  • Hinge thickness: approximately 10% of diameter

Phase 3 — Retreat

  • The moment the tree begins to fall: step back and move along your pre-planned escape route
  • Eyes on the upper trunk and crown — watch for debris and unexpected movement
  • Do NOT turn your back on the falling tree
  • Move immediately — do not pause to pick up tools

After the Fall

  • Wait until the tree has fully come to rest and crown bounce has stopped
  • Check for widow makers dislodged during fall
  • Give the all-clear signal before crew re-enters the zone
  • Assess the stump: did the hinge work as designed? Debrief with crew.

⬡ Hinge Wood — Reference Targets

Open face notch (70° opening angle) allows the backcut to be positioned above the notch apex, giving more control over fall direction and reducing barber-chair risk compared to the conventional notch.
Parameter Conventional Notch Open Face (Recommended)
Opening angle 45° or less 70°+
Notch depth 25–35% of diameter 70–80% of diameter
Backcut vs. notch Backcut meets notch apex (risk of hinge failure) Backcut above notch apex — lower hinge breakage risk
Barber chair risk Higher — conventional notch can split upward Significantly lower
Hinge control Moderate Excellent — more predictable break-point

Per TCIA Ground Worker Safety (2nd Ed.) and ISA BMP Safe Work Practices: the open face notch is the recommended standard for all professional felling work due to reduced barber-chair risk and superior hinge control.

⬡ Pre-Felling Checklist

Use at every felling operation. All items must be checked before the saw starts.

  • Tree lean assessed from two axes
  • Widow makers and overhead hazards identified
  • Decay/structural defects noted and factored into strategy
  • Felling direction confirmed — targets and structures cleared
  • Two escape routes cleared of obstructions (10 m minimum)
  • Crew briefed — everyone knows their position and escape route
  • Two-tree-length exclusion zone established and cleared
  • Spotter designated
  • Communication signals agreed (verbal/hand)
  • Wedges and tools staged outside exclusion zone
  • Chainsaw inspected — chain tension, chain brake, bar oil
  • All PPE in place: hard hat, face shield, chaps, gloves, boots
⚠ WorkSafeBC Requirements — Solo Felling

Per WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation Part 26, felling operations require at minimum two workers present — one to operate the saw and one as designated observer/spotter. Solo felling of trees over a specified diameter is prohibited without specific risk assessment and written safe work procedure approval from WorkSafeBC. When in doubt: two workers is the standard.
