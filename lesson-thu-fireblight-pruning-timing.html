Fire Blight (Erwinia amylovora): When to Cut, and Why Timing Matters

Fire blight is a bacterium that hides in cankers over winter and comes out with the spring warmth. Cut at the wrong time, the wrong distance or the wrong way and you spread it. Here's what the research says.

⏱ About 10 minutes · Week 19 · Microbe Tree Inc. crew training

🔥 The short answer

The research says the opposite of "don't prune blighted trees while dormant." Winter is the best time to cut out fire blight cankers. The bacteria are inactive, they stay inside the canker, and every canker you remove is one less source of ooze in spring. What winter is not for on an infected tree is heavy pruning for size or shape. That pushes out a flush of soft shoots in spring, and soft shoots are exactly what fire blight attacks. That's probably where the "don't prune it dormant" idea comes from.

Question 1 of 5
Where does the fire blight bacterium spend the winter, and what wakes it up?
Cankers are the winter hideout

The bacteria overwinter in living tissue at the margins of cankers from earlier infections. As temperatures rise in spring they become active again, and in warm, moist weather they multiply quickly. Virginia Tech cites activity resuming above about 10 °C, with optimum growth around 18–29 °C. They then ooze out of the canker edges as sticky, amber droplets loaded with bacteria.

  • Blossom infection needs open flowers, warmth and a wetting event (rain or dew). The MaryBlyt risk model uses an average daily temperature above about 15.6 °C as one of its conditions.
  • Not every canker reactivates, but one active canker can release huge numbers of bacteria.
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Okanagan orchards follow fire blight risk models every spring because warm, humid spells at bloom set off infections. The same weather hits the crabapples, pears and hawthorns in strata beds next door.

Question 2 of 5
Which of these is not a main way fire blight spreads in spring and summer?
Rain, insects, flowers, wounds and tools

Fire blight is a bacterium. It doesn't make spores and doesn't infect conifers; it's limited to the rose family. The bacteria move through:

  • Rain splash and wind, carrying ooze from cankers onto blossoms and shoots
  • Insects drawn to the ooze, then pollinators spreading it from flower to flower
  • Wounds from hail, storms and pruning cuts, plus natural openings in soft shoots
  • Tools during the growing season, when the bacteria are active

Once inside, they move down shoots toward older wood, producing the shepherd's crook tip and leaves that stay hanging on dead twigs.

Question 3 of 5
Why do extension services (Clemson, WSU, the BC Tree Fruit Production Guide) recommend removing cankers during the dormant season?
Winter: take the inoculum out
  • Bacteria are inactive and stay inside the canker. WSU notes that winter cuts can be made closer to the visible canker edge than summer cuts.
  • Removing inoculum. Every canker cut out in winter is one less ooze source at bloom. UMass calls dormant removal of infected wood "critical."
  • Little tool spread. WSU, Penn State and Utah State say tools don't need sterilizing between cuts on fully dormant trees. Sanitizing anyway is cheap insurance.
  • Easy to spot. Blighted branches often hold dead leaves into winter.
📐 BC Tree Fruit Production Guide: cut overwintering cankers out during the dormant season, 15–30 cm below the canker margin. Remove both smooth- and cracked-margin cankers, and go over susceptible trees more than once because cankers are easy to miss.
📋 Field Note — Microbe Tree Crew

On a dormant job, do the blight cuts first, bag or chip that material off site, then do any structural work. Same order WSU recommends for orchards.

Question 4 of 5
It's July, dry and hot, and a client's crabapple has fresh strikes with shepherd's-crook tips. What's the evidence-based move?
Summer strikes: deep cuts, dry weather, ugly stub

Active strikes can race down into scaffolds and the trunk, so most guides say remove them promptly, but only in dry weather. The bacteria run ahead of what you can see:

  • Cut-back distance: the BC guide says at least 30 cm below visible disease. WSU's best practice is 30–45 cm (12–18 in) into two-year-old or older wood.
  • "Ugly stub": a method credited to Paul Steiner. Instead of cutting back to the collar, leave a bare stub of about 10–15 cm (4–6 in) in older wood and mark it. Any new canker forms in the stub, which you remove properly in winter. WSU trials found stubs reduced canker re-formation on structural wood in some experiments but not others.
  • Weather: the BC guide says avoid wet weather or storms expected within 24 hours.
  • Too many strikes? Wisconsin Extension advises waiting until dormancy rather than heavy summer cutting.

Tool sanitation: 10% bleach or 70% alcohol between cuts is the standard advice, and the BC guide stresses it when cuts are under 30 cm from disease. WSU trials found sanitizing made little difference when cuts were deep enough. Distance matters most; sanitize anyway.

Question 5 of 5
Where does the "don't prune infected trees while dormant" idea have a real point?
Remove the blight, not half the tree

Several sources warn about vigour:

  • Cornell: vigorous shoot growth is highly susceptible; manage vigour with balanced nutrition and avoid excessive winter pruning.
  • Penn State: after heavy pruning, trees push hard in spring. If any cankers are left, bacteria move into that new growth.
  • Ohio State and UMN: heavy pruning and excess nitrogen promote susceptible succulent growth. Ohio State says never combine routine shaping pruning with blight removal.

So the fair version of that rule is: dormant season = remove cankers, yes; heavy structural or size-reduction pruning on a blight-prone tree = spread it over several years. We found no credible source that advises against removing cankers while trees are dormant.

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Strata crabapples, ornamental pears and hawthorns that get topped or hard-sheared every winter often come back as a mass of water sprouts, which are prime shoot blight targets. Recommend light annual thinning, suckers removed, and no late-season nitrogen.
