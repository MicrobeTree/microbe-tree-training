Oystershell Scale (Lepidosaphes ulmi): Timing Is Everything

Eggs under armour all winter, crawlers for a few weeks in late spring. Where dormant oil fits, where it falls short, and what to look for while you prune.

🐚 The short answer

Oystershell scale (Lepidosaphes ulmi) is an armoured scale that looks like a tiny oyster shell glued to the bark. Right now, in November, it's sitting on the bark as eggs under the dead mother's shell. It's only exposed and easy to hit for a short window as crawlers in late spring. That timing decides when a treatment works and when it's wasted.

Female ≈ 3 mm
Eggs under the mother's scale
Late May–June
1 per year in the Northwest

🌳 Hosts you'll see in Okanagan yards

  • NRCan (Canada-wide): apple, white ash, white elm, basswood and red maple. Among shrubs, lilac is perhaps the most often infested.
  • Utah State (a dry-West guide): over 128 hosts, worst on lilac, aspen, ash, cotoneaster, willow, poplar and maple. Colorado State lists the same group.
  • NRCan: infestations are common in ornamental plantings where trees are stressed. In our climate, that means drought-stressed yard trees.

🔍 What it looks like and what it does

  • Curved, oyster-shaped scales on trunks, limbs and twigs. NRCan and Utah State call them brownish grey, while the BC home garden guide says whitish. They blend into bark, so look closely.
  • They suck sap, which slows growth, dries and cracks the bark, and causes twig and branch dieback. Bark can be completely encrusted, and some trees die (NRCan, Utah State).

📅 Life cycle

  • Winter: eggs under the mother's cover. NRCan says 50–60 per scale, and WSU says 40–100. WSU: they spend almost 75% of their lives as eggs protected by the scale.
  • Crawlers: CSU says late May or early June, mostly over 2–3 weeks. WSU says May–June. NRCan says about mid-June. The BC guide says June. Weather shifts it every year.
  • Crawlers are the only mobile stage (CSU). Within days they settle, lose their legs and start building the waxy cover. After that, most insecticides work much less well.
  • Eggs are laid in late summer and early fall, and the mother dies (CSU, NRCan).

🛢️ Dormant oil: when it helps, when it doesn't

  • Helps: the BC home garden guide's management advice for fruit-tree scales says to apply dormant oil in late winter or early spring, up to the pre-pink stage, with thorough coverage. It adds that trees given dormant oil every spring seldom have scale problems.
  • Limits: CSU says dormant oil can kill many overwintering eggs, but the thick waxy cover means oil may not penetrate to kill all of them. In outbreaks, add other controls.
  • Doesn't help when: it's too cold (the BC guide's general dormant oil notes say don't apply it below 4°C), coverage is poor, or it's on a sensitive tree (BC: dormant oil may damage the bark of 'Red Delicious' apple).
  • Crawler window: CSU calls crawlers highly vulnerable, and timing is critical. To find them, wrap double-sided sticky tape around infested twigs and check with a hand lens (BC, Utah State).
  • Wrong tool: Utah State says imidacloprid is ineffective against hard scales.
  • No spray at all: the same BC scale advice says to scrape scales off, prune out heavily infested branches, and consider removing old, encrusted trees.

📋 Field Note: dormant pruning season

You'll be in lilacs, ash, aspen, poplars and fruit trees all winter. While you prune, look at the bark. Dieback plus little grey oyster shells means pruning out the worst branches now, and flagging the client for a late-winter oil spray and a crawler check in late May–June.

Question 1 of 5
It's November. What stage is oystershell scale in on a lilac right now?
Eggs under armour
  • CSU: winter is spent in the egg stage under the old cover of the mother scale.
  • NRCan: eggs overwinter in groups of 50 to 60 under the female scale. WSU says 40–100.
Question 2 of 5
When is oystershell scale most vulnerable to treatment?
Hit the crawlers
  • CSU: crawlers are highly vulnerable because they lack the waxy cover. Treatment is much less effective after the crawlers have settled and molted.
  • WSU: the insect is most vulnerable in June, when the newly hatched young are crawling.
  • Monitor with double-sided sticky tape rather than guessing the date.
Question 3 of 5
A client asks if one dormant oil spray will wipe out a heavy infestation. What's the accurate answer?
Useful, not a silver bullet
  • CSU: dormant oil can kill many overwintering eggs, but oils may not penetrate to kill all. During outbreaks it usually needs to be supplemented.
  • BC guide (scale management): scrape off scales and prune out heavily infested branches.
Question 4 of 5
According to the BC home garden guide, which condition rules out a dormant oil spray?
Watch the thermometer
  • BC guide: do not apply dormant oil when temperatures are below 4°C. Apply it in late winter or early spring, up to pre-pink, with thorough coverage.
  • It may also damage the bark of 'Red Delicious' apple.
