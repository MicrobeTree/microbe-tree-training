🌲 Austrian pine (Pinus nigra)

A dark, stiff-needled pine used as a specimen, screen or windbreak (NC State), and one you'll want to recognize on Kelowna and West Kelowna properties. It's not native (central and southern Europe, per NC State). The City of Kelowna's urban tree guide lists it as a conifer for full sun, moderate water, pollution tolerant and deer resistant. NC State adds that it tolerates clay soil, urban pollution, salt and drought once established. The catch: it's also the pine most prone to Diplodia tip blight, and drought stress is exactly what lets the disease take hold.

In bundles of 2, stiff, sharp, dark green, 3–6 in (about 8–15 cm)
Dark gray to black, furrowed, plate-like
Groups of 2–4, point out away from the stem
50–60 ft tall (15–18 m), 20–40 ft spread (6–12 m)
📐 City of Kelowna's urban tree guide: Austrian pine = Sun F, Water M (moderate). The same guide says all trees need more watering during prolonged drought (over 10 days), applied at the outer edge of the branches, where the fine roots are.
Don't mix up"Drought tolerant" means once established. A drought-stressed Austrian pine is the textbook set-up for Diplodia (NRCan).

🍂 Diplodia tip blight in a nutshell

  • Cause: the fungus Diplodia sapinea (older name Sphaeropsis sapinea). NRCan: it's endemic to North America, has been noted in BC, and hits two- and three-needle pines growing outside their natural range hardest. Austrian pine is the most susceptible, Scots pine a close second. In western Canada the native pines it mainly affects are lodgepole and ponderosa.
  • Symptoms: expanding candles stunted, yellowing, then tan to brown (the whole current-season shoot); lower branches first; resin droplets; later tiny black fruiting bodies at the base of the needles and on cone scales (Morton Arboretum, Purdue).
  • Stress link: NRCan: drought, hail and other wounds both open the door and push a latent infection into disease. Repeated years of infection can kill trees of all ages.
  • Fungicide: Morton: the first treatment goes on at budbreak. NRCan: it may be warranted on high-value non-native ornamentals; check Health Canada's Pesticide Product Information Database for what's currently registered, and use a licensed applicator where required.
Question 1 of 5
A West Kelowna client asks what pine is in her front yard. The needles are in bundles of two, 10–12 cm long, stiff and sharply pointed, the bark is dark and plated, and cones stick straight out. Best identification?
Quick ID: two stiff needles per bundle
  • NC State quick ID: needles in bundles of two, stiff, rigid, sharply pointed; dark brown to black furrowed bark; cones in groups of 2–4 pointing out away from the stem.
  • Needles are 3–6 inches long and are kept up to 4 years. Good ID habit: count needles per bundle first.
  • It's not native (central and southern Europe, per NC State). NC State flags that it's often confused with ponderosa pine (Pinus ponderosa), so check the needles and cones, not just the bark.

Question 2 of 5
A homeowner says: "Austrian pine is drought tolerant, so I never need to water it." What do you say?
Tolerant ≠ self-sufficient
  • City of Kelowna: the water column describes general needs once established and for a typical season; all trees need more water during prolonged drought (over 10 days), applied at the outer edge of the branches.
  • NRCan: drought can cause heavy levels of Diplodia, both by stressing trees (so latent infections turn into disease) and by opening wounds.
  • Extension guides (Purdue, Morton): water the root zone in dry spells, not with an overhead sprinkler that wets the foliage (Purdue), mulch (keep it off the trunk), and avoid heavy nitrogen, which has been linked to worse disease.
Question 3 of 5
Late June. An Austrian pine's lower branches have stunted, tan-brown new shoots, resin droplets on the twigs, and tiny black dots at the base of some needles. Most likely diagnosis?
Reading the signs
  • Morton Arboretum: new candles stunted, yellow, then tan or brown; resin droplets from cankers; black fruiting bodies at the base of the needle under the sheath; symptoms often start on the lower half of the tree and progress up.
  • Wisconsin: the resin flow differs from boring-insect damage because there's no tunneling.
  • Purdue (Midwest timing, ours will differ): symptoms on new shoots often show in spring, dieback through early summer; infection happens while the young needles are elongating in wet spring weather.
📋 Field Note: Okanagan

Look at the lower crown first when you walk up. It's easy to blame a dry summer, but the black dots and resin tell you it's the fungus and the drought together.

Question 4 of 5
You're asked to clean out dead tips on an infected Austrian pine. Which plan matches the extension advice?
Sanitation and timing
  • Purdue: prune out visibly infected branches during dry weather, remove infected cones, disinfect tools between cuts (70% rubbing alcohol), remove fallen needles and cones; avoid pruning in spring and early summer because the fungus also infects wounds.
  • Morton: cones and dead tips carry the fruiting bodies, so remove and destroy them when feasible.
  • Wisconsin: don't prune in wet weather; treat tools at least 30 seconds with 70% alcohol or a 10% bleach solution, and rinse and oil afterward.
  • How far back? The guides differ: Wisconsin says cut 6–8 in (about 15–20 cm) below obvious infection, Ohio State says at least 2 ft (about 60 cm) below visible symptoms. Cut back into clearly healthy wood and know the numbers vary by source.
Question 5 of 5
A client with a diseased Austrian pine wants a replacement pine. Which statement is accurate?
Resistance vs site
  • Morton Arboretum: Diplodia attacks pines with needles in bunches of 2 and 3. Austrian pine is the most susceptible; Scots, red, mugo and ponderosa are also susceptible. Pines with needles in bunches of 5 are highly resistant.
  • City of Kelowna's urban tree list includes western white pine and eastern white pine (five-needle species), plus ponderosa and Scotch pine. Check the sun and water ratings and the site before you recommend one.
  • Kelowna also says to avoid monocultures; diversity reduces pest problems.
