Reaction Wood: How Trees Fight a Lean

Hardwoods pull, conifers push. Read reaction wood and you'll read the lean, the risk, and Friday's barber chair before it happens.

⏱ About 10 minutes · Week 20 · Microbe Tree Inc. crew training

🌲 The short answer

A tree can't un-bend old wood, so when it leans it lays down special reaction wood to push or pull itself back toward upright. Hardwoods pull from the top with tension wood. Conifers push from underneath with compression wood. Same job, opposite sides.

Tension wood, UPPER side of the lean
Compression wood, LOWER side of the lean
Off-centre pith, wide rings on the reaction side
A leaner is a loaded spring. That's barber chair.
🧠 Memory hook: "Hardwoods hang on from the top, conifers kick from the bottom."
Question 1 of 5
A Douglas-fir on a Peachland slope has been leaning for 20 years. Where will you find its reaction wood?
Conifers push from below

In conifers (gymnosperms) reaction wood forms on the bottom side of a leaning stem or branch and is called compression wood. It pushes the stem upright (Groover 2016; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 3).

  • Cells: shorter, rounded tracheids with thick, lignin-rich walls.
  • Look at the stump: the pith is off-centre and the rings are much wider on the underside.
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Our slopes are full of ponderosa pine and Douglas-fir that have corrected a lean. Look at the stump on your next removal: wide, dark rings on the downhill or lean side tell you the tree has been fighting gravity for years.

Question 2 of 5
A cottonwood leans over a Kelowna strata roof. Where is its reaction wood, and what does it do?
Hardwoods pull from the top

In hardwoods (angiosperms) reaction wood is tension wood, formed on the upper side of a leaning stem. Its fibres carry a special cellulose-rich inner wall, the gelatinous layer (G-layer), and they generate a strong contractile pull that draws the stem upright (Groover 2016).

  • Populus (cottonwood, aspen, poplar) is the model tree scientists use to study tension wood.
  • The stump shows wider rings on the upper side.
Question 3 of 5
You're cutting firewood rounds from a leaned-over Siberian elm. The slabs from the upper side of the lean bow and twist badly as they dry. Why?
Reaction wood is a lumber defect

The USDA Wood Handbook and Mississippi State's wood-drying chapter both flag reaction wood as a defect in lumber:

  • It shrinks more along the grain than normal wood, so boards bow, warp and check.
  • Restrained compression wood can even fracture across the grain (crossbreaks) as it dries (Mississippi State).

For us it's mostly a reading tool: it tells you the tree has been under sustained load on one side.

Question 4 of 5
What does reaction wood tell you during a tree risk assessment?
Old lean vs new lean
  • Corrected, old lean: top curves back toward vertical, reaction wood in the stem. The tree has adapted to its lean over years.
  • New lean: straight stem tipped over, soil heaving or cracking on the side away from the lean, exposed roots. That points to root or soil failure and needs immediate attention.

The BC Forest Safety Council's heavy leaner procedure says the same thing from the faller's side: ask why the tree is leaning, check for top correction, ground cracks and root decay, because a recent lean can mean a compromised root system.

📋 ISA exam

Tree Risk (11%) and Tree Biology (11%) both use this. Know: angiosperm = tension wood = upper side; gymnosperm = compression wood = lower side.

Question 5 of 5
Why does any of this matter to a faller on Friday?
Loaded spring

Every leaning tree carries its weight as tension on the back (upper) side and compression on the front. Reaction wood is the tree adding even more pull or push to the same picture.

When a conventional back cut slowly releases a heavy leaner, the trunk can split upward from the back cut and the butt can kick back toward the faller. That's a barber chair, and it's Friday's lesson.

📋 Field Note — Microbe Tree Crew

Before you cut any leaner, read the lean like a load: which side is stretched, which side is squeezed, and what happens when you cut it.
