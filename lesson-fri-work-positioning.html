🧗 Worksite Techniques — Week 10

Work Positioning: Lanyards & Secondary Attachment

Once you're in the tree, your positioning lanyard is what keeps you stable, comfortable, and safe to work. It's the second attachment — the one that lets you free up your climbing system to redirect while you're still connected. Using it right is fundamental. Using it wrong is how climbers get hurt.

📋
ANSI Z133 §7.4.2 — Two Points of Attachment

"Climbers shall maintain at least one point of attachment to the tree at all times while working aloft." In practice: you do not disconnect your climbing line until your lanyard is secured. You do not release your lanyard until your climbing line is re-established or you've transferred to a new anchor. Double-up, don't drop first. This isn't a suggestion — it's the standard.

🎯 Work Positioning vs. Fall Arrest — Two Very Different Systems

This distinction is foundational and appears on the ISA exam. The terms sound similar but the engineering and regulatory frameworks are entirely different.

Feature Work Positioning System Fall Arrest System
Purpose Holds climber against structure for hands-free work; allows positioning adjustment Arrests a free fall; stops a falling worker
Free fall permitted? No — must be taut or under light tension at all times. Free fall in a positioning system = shock load to the system and climber = injury risk Yes — designed to arrest falls; energy absorbers reduce peak force
In arboriculture Fliplines, adjustable positioning lanyards, Prusik on a flipline tail Ground-based fall arrest lanyards, PFAS in aerial lift work
ANSI standard ANSI Z133 (arborist operations); ANSI Z359.2 (general industry) ANSI Z359.1 (shock-absorbing lanyards); Z359.3 (self-retracting devices)
Key limitation NOT a substitute for fall arrest when a free fall of >0.6m can occur without any other attachment. Cannot be the sole means of protection in certain aerial lift applications. More restrictive to movement; not designed for repositioning during climbing operations
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

When you're in a ponderosa at a Westbank property and you need to work out on a scaffold branch, your positioning lanyard (flipline) goes around the branch or(the trunk, keeping you pressed against the wood while you cut. That's work positioning. If you were in an aerial lift platform instead, the requirements under ANSI Z133 and WorkSafeBC would specify fall arrest equipment connected to the platform anchor point — a different system for a different context. Know which system you're in and what it's designed to do.

🔗 Lanyard Types — What's in Your Gear Bag

Not all lanyards are equal. The application determines the correct lanyard type. Understanding the trade-offs helps you pick the right tool and inspect it correctly.

🔵 Wire-Core Flipline

Steel cable core (typically 7×7 or 7×19 wire rope) covered in rope or polyester cover. Provides cut resistance if chainsaw contacts the lanyard. Standard issue for chainsaw operations aloft.

Strengths
Chainsaw cut resistance Durable Predictable stretch
Limitations
Heavy Stiffer handling Inspect terminations carefully for broken wires
🟢 Rope / Dyneema Flipline

Arborist rope or HMPE (Dyneema/Spectra) braid. Lighter, more tactile feel, excellent strength-to-weight ratio. Used in non-chainsaw climbing work or where climber weight savings matter.

Strengths
Lightweight Excellent grip feel Flexible
Limitations
No chainsaw protection HMPE: UV degradation Check cover abrasion regularly
🟡 Adjustable Rope Lanyard

Uses an integrated sliding hitch adjuster (built-in Prusik or cam mechanism) to allow length adjustment without unhooking. Common on adjustable flipline systems.

Strengths
Adjustable length Versatile No separate Prusik needed
Limitations
Cam systems can fail if dirty Inspect adjuster mechanism each use
🟣 Prusik Loop on Flipline

A Prusik hitch tied on the flipline or a separate positioning lanyard tail. Allows hands-free adjustability when weighted and easy movement when unweighted. Classic, reliable, field-replaceable.

Strengths
Simple and reliable Easy to inspect Replace in field
Limitations
Requires correct cord diameter match Check for glazing / melting

🪢 Saddle Connection Points — What Goes Where

Where you connect your positioning lanyard to your saddle matters. Most modern arborist saddles have multiple connection points, and each has a specific purpose and rating.

Connection PointPurposeLanyard Connection OK?Notes
Bridge / central D-ring Primary climbing system attachment Context-dependent — check saddle manual Some saddles spec the bridge for primary climbing only; use side D for lanyard
Side D-rings (hip) Work positioning lanyards, tool lanyards, secondary attachments Yes — primary lanyard connection point Connecting lanyard to both side D-rings distributes load; single D-ring also acceptable per most saddle specs
Leg loops Structural comfort; not load-bearing connection points No — not rated as connection points Never attach load-bearing equipment directly to leg loop buckles or fabric
Tool loops / rings Tool lanyards, pouches No — rated for tools only, not body weight Tool rings are typically rated 25kg max — not for climber weight

Carabiner requirements for lanyard connections: Per ANSI Z133 and saddle manufacturer specifications, carabiners used in life-safety positioning connections must be:

  • Rated minimum 23 kN in the major axis (most NFPA/ANSI-rated oval and D-shape carabiners meet this)
  • Equipped with a locking gate — three-action or screw-lock. Auto-locking gates are preferred for positions where unintentional opening is possible.
  • Inspected before each use: gate action, lock function, no cracks, corrosion, deformation, or gate play
  • Oriented correctly: major axis loaded; gate not resting against any edge or object that could cause unintentional opening

✅ Pre-Use Inspection Checklist — Lanyard & Positioning System

Per ANSI Z133 §5.2, all equipment shall be inspected before each use. This checklist covers the lanyard and its connection hardware. Work through it before going up — not after.

Rope / Cover: No cuts, abrasion flats, glazing, stiffness, or discolouration along full length. Cover is intact — no core visible. Ends are properly whipped or heat-sealed.
Wire core (if wire-core lanyard): Inspect both end terminations carefully — bend the cover back and look for broken wires at the swage or splice. Any broken wires = retire the lanyard immediately. IWRC wire rope: retire if 3 or more broken wires in one lay, or 6 in one strand per lay.
Hardware ends (snap hooks / shackles): Gate opens and closes freely. Gate locking mechanism engages fully and does not rattle or stick. No visible cracks, corrosion pitting, or deformation in the body or gate.
Splices / terminations: If rope ends are spliced (eye splice), the splice is tight, no strands pulling out, no separation at the throat. Thimble (if present) is seated and not cracked.
Prusik / friction hitch (if used): Hitch cord has no glazing, melting, or core damage. Diameter is appropriate for host rope (Prusik cord should be 60–80% of host rope diameter). Hitch grips immediately when weighted.
Connecting carabiner: Lock engaged. Gate action smooth. Body and gate: no cracks, pitting, or deformation. Major axis orientation noted — carabiner will be loaded on major axis, not cross-loaded.
Length / adjustment: Lanyard length appropriate for work planned. Too long = potential for slack and unexpected load; too short = restricted movement. Adjustable systems: adjuster mechanism functions correctly.
Date of last formal inspection: All life-safety equipment must be formally inspected by a competent person at minimum annually per ANSI Z133. Check company records. Equipment with no inspection history should not be used.

⚠️ Common Errors — Don't Make These

❌ Drop First, Double-Up Second

Releasing the climbing line before the lanyard is secured around a new anchor. Results in a moment — sometimes more — with only one or zero points of attachment. This is the most common critical error in positioning transitions.

✓ Correct Approach

Set the positioning lanyard first — around the trunk, over the limb, wherever it needs to go — and confirm it's loaded before releasing the climbing system. Double-up, then drop. Every time.

❌ Lanyard Routed Beneath Included Bark Union

Looping a lanyard through a tight co-dominant fork with included bark. The union is structurally compromised; dynamic loading on the lanyard can initiate or worsen the failure. If that union lets go while you're hanging on it, you're falling.

✓ Correct Approach

Assess every lanyard anchor point before committing your weight. No included bark, no decay at the union, adequate diameter for the load. If the anchor is questionable, find a better one — even if it means an extra move.

❌ Unlocked Carabiner Gate

Running a lanyard on a snap hook or carabiner with the gate unlocked. Gates can open on contact with bark, branch stubs, or knot bodies. An open gate under load can invert and roll out — resulting in a completely failed connection.

✓ Correct Approach

Lock every gate, every time, before loading. It takes one second. Make it automatic. Consider three-action auto-lock gates for positioning carabiners that are constantly being attached and detached.

❌ Running Saw With Only Lanyard Attached

Operating a chainsaw with only the positioning lanyard securing the climber — climbing system not established or previously unclipped. If the saw bucks, the climber reacts, and there is nothing to arrest a fall if the lanyard fails or the anchor breaks.

✓ Correct Approach

Per ANSI Z133 §7.4.7: climbers operating chainsaws aloft must maintain two points of attachment. One positioning, one climbing system (or two positioning systems in some configurations). No exceptions for "quick cuts."

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Most serious climbing injuries and fatalities in arboriculture don't happen because of faulty equipment. They happen because of a momentary lapse in attachment protocol — the split-second decision to "just reach over there" without resetting the lanyard first. Building the double-up habit takes deliberate practice. The best crews make it automatic: you don't think about it, you just do it, every transition, every time. New climbers: watch how your lead climber moves through the tree. If they're doing it right, you'll see them set the lanyard before they release anything else. If they're not — that's a conversation worth having before the next job.

⚠ WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation Part 26

BC WorkSafeBC regulations for tree work require that all fall protection equipment used in arborist operations meet CSA or equivalent standards and be used in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Employers are required to provide a written fall protection plan where workers may fall more than 3 metres. The positioning lanyard system and its components must be documented as part of that plan. Per BC OHS Reg §26.2(2), it is the employer's obligation to ensure workers are trained and competent in use of the equipment before they use it.
