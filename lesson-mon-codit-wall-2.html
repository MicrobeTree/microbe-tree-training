CODIT Part 2: Wall 2, the Growth-Ring Wall

How trees try to keep decay out of the core, why deep cuts beat it, and what a rotten centre means when you climb, rig or fell.

🌳 CODIT series, part 2 of 4

  • Last Monday: Wall 1 plugs the vertical pipes and is the weakest wall, so decay runs long up and down the stem.
  • Today: Wall 2 resists decay moving inward, toward the centre (pith).
  • Coming up: Wall 3 (around the stem) and Wall 4 (the barrier zone).

🧱 Wall 2: the growth-ring wall

  • Job: resists decay spreading inward toward the pith, behind the wound (Shigo 1984; Penn State).
  • Where: Bartlett says it's formed by the last cells in each growth ring and runs around each ring except where rays pass through. Penn State says it develops in the latewood and resists with chemical compounds. Mississippi State calls it a wall of thickened cells.
  • Strength: Shigo calls it moderately strong, and Bartlett ranks it the second weakest (stronger than Wall 1, weaker than Walls 3 and 4).
  • Its weak spot is depth. Shigo: when shallow wounds kill cambium, Wall 2 resists the inward spread behind them. When wounds go deeper toward older wood, its resisting power diminishes. When Wall 2 fails all the way to the pith, the inward spread ends there, and the decay now occupies the core.

⚖️ Sources differ on the anatomy. Bartlett and Penn State tie Wall 2 to the growth-ring boundary and latewood. Santamour (ISA's Journal of Arboriculture) says Wall 2 is not simply the cells at the ring border, but an irregular, connected patchwork of plugged cells. For the exam, remember "Wall 2 = inward, growth rings".

🌲 Strong vs weak compartmentalizers

  • Santamour rates trees as "strong" or "weak" compartmentalizers by how well they resist inward spread to the pith, which is Wall 2's job.
  • Penn State: oak, hickory, sugar maple and redwood are strong. Silver maple, cottonwood and willow are weak and "try to outgrow the column of decay" while their centres go hollow.
  • Cottonwood and willow line every creek and lakeshore job we do. Expect hollow centres in them.
⚠️ Hazards of Wall 2: rot in the coreOnce Wall 2 lets decay reach the pith, you can have a central decay column: a stem that looks normal outside but is soft or hollow in the middle. Shigo: hollows form when Wall 3 also fails over time. The USDA climbing guide notes trees usually decay from the inside out, while sound wood keeps being added outside.

🪵 Where to expect it

  • Old topping cuts. Smith 2006 (USDA Forest Service): stems have no preformed boundaries along their length. Combined with less-responsive heartwood in the core, extensive spread of decay is likely after topping.
  • Flush cuts. Smith 2006: a flush cut removes the branch protection zone, makes a larger wound and injures the stem itself, putting the wound right against the stem's own rings.
  • Big, deep wounds (old large cuts, splits, equipment damage) reach older rings where Shigo says Wall 2 resists less.

🧗 Tie-ins & rigging

  • A hollow leader can carry its own weight and still fail under climber or rigging load. The USDA climbing guide says trees with a sound outer shell may be relatively safe, but it depends on the ratio of sound to decayed wood. If you have doubts, avoid it.
  • TCIA: in decayed trees, climb in short steps and check for decay well below the tie-in point, because your line bends the stem below it.
  • One hazard-rating rule of thumb (forestpathology.org): at least 1/3 of the radius should be sound shell. That's for rating trees, not a climbing standard, so don't bet a tie-in on it.

🪓 Felling

  • A central column can put rot right where your hinge needs to be. Husqvarna: if you suspect rot, make an inspection bore through the hinge area, look for discoloured, soft wood, keep the hinge in healthy wood, and fell in the natural direction or use ropes if the hinge is weak.
📋 Field Note: our pruning cuts

You're reducing an old topped leader and the round you drop shows a dark ring of sound wood around a brown, soft centre. That's Wall 2 losing. Now think about the cuts we make. A small cut at the branch collar exposes young, shallow wood. A big heading cut through a thick leader exposes many rings, including the old inner ones, where Shigo says Wall 2 is weakest. Mississippi State: minimize wound size. Make the smaller cut when you have the choice, and never flush.

Question 1 of 5
Which direction does CODIT Wall 2 resist decay?
Wall 2 = inward
  • Shigo (1984): Wall 1 vertical, Wall 2 inward (radial), Wall 3 lateral (tangential). Wall 4 separates old wood from new.
  • Penn State: Wall 2 resists decay spreading inward toward the center.
Question 2 of 5
Where do Bartlett and Penn State say Wall 2 forms?
Growth rings
  • Bartlett: Wall 2 is formed by the last cells in each growth ring.
  • Penn State: it develops in latewood.
  • Santamour cautions it isn't simply the ring-border cells but a connected patchwork of plugged cells. Know the debate exists.
Question 3 of 5
According to Shigo, what weakens Wall 2?
Depth beats Wall 2
  • Shigo (1984): Wall 2 resists inward spread behind shallow wounds, but if wounds are inflicted more deeply and approach older wood, its resisting power diminishes.
  • That's why big, deep cuts and flush cuts (which injure the stem itself) matter.

Question 4 of 5
You're about to tie in to a cottonwood leader above an old topping cut. Sounding gives a dull thud. What's the call?
Weak compartmentalizer + old topping
  • Penn State: cottonwood, silver maple and willow are weak compartmentalizers whose centres go hollow.
  • Smith 2006: after topping, extensive spread of decay is likely.
  • TCIA: check decay well below the TIP. USDA climbing guide: if decay is evident and you have doubts, avoid it.
Question 5 of 5
You can remove a big leader with one large heading cut, or make a smaller cut at a branch collar. Why does CODIT favour the smaller cut?
Minimize the wound
  • Shigo: deeper wounds toward older wood mean Wall 2's resisting power diminishes.
  • Mississippi State: wound size matters for closure, so minimize wound size when pruning.
  • Smith 2006: cut just outside the branch collar, not flush and not leaving long stubs.
