Cooley Spruce Gall Adelgid: Spruce Galls, Douglas-fir Fluff

A two-host insect: cone-shaped galls on spruce, white woolly flecks on Douglas-fir, and a lot of homeowner worry for not much damage.

🐛 Cooley spruce gall adelgid (Adelges cooleyi)

A tiny, soft-bodied insect (adults under 2 mm, related to aphids) that gives spruce those cone-shaped galls on the branch tips, and gives Douglas-fir a white, woolly look with yellowed, bent needles. NRCan says it's native to western North America, including BC, and it moves between two hosts: spruce (primary) and Douglas-fir (secondary).

Galls about 5 cm long on new tips: green, then reddish purple; dry galls open like mouths
White woolly flecks; needles discoloured, distorted. No galls
2 years if both hosts are present (NRCan)
Mostly cosmetic; not a major pest (NRCan, CSU)
📐 Spruce hosts in NRCan's list include Colorado blue, white, Engelmann and Sitka spruce. The City of Kelowna's urban tree list names blue spruce and Interior Douglas-fir, so both hosts are plantable in our area. Know them together.
Don't mix upThe galls are not cones. CSU: they're "often mistaken for seed cones." Cones hang from the branch; galls sit on the shoot tip and are made by the tree reacting to the insect.

🔄 The two-host cycle

  • Spring: the overwintered female (the fundatrix) on spruce matures and lays a large clutch of eggs near the opening buds. Feeding makes the tree form a gall around the nymphs (NRCan, Penn State).
  • Mid-summer: galls dry out and turn brown, the chambers open, and winged adults emerge. Many fly to Douglas-fir to lay eggs; the abandoned galls stay on the tree (CSU).
  • On Douglas-fir: nymphs overwinter dormant, mature in spring and reproduce asexually; winged females head back to spruce in late summer to complete the cycle (NRCan).
  • Source disagreement: NRCan gives a two-year cycle when both hosts are present; CSU says it often takes a year. Penn State: at least two years for all five forms. Where only spruce is present (Prairies), it reproduces asexually (NRCan).
Question 1 of 5
A client points to purple-brown, pine-cone-looking growths on the tips of her blue spruce and asks if it's a cone crop. What is it?
Gall, not cone
  • NRCan: cone-shaped galls about 5 cm long with sharp projections on new growth at the branch tips of spruce; green at first, eventually reddish purple; as they dry, mouth-shaped openings appear and the dry galls can stay on the tree for several years.
  • CSU: galls are often mistaken for seed cones, and old ones get covered by new growth.
  • Penn State: the gall is an elongate, pineapple-shaped growth on the tip of the current year's twig.

Galls are plant tissue distortions caused by an organism manipulating the host. Diagnosis & Treatment (9%) starts with "what is causing it, and what host is it on?"

Question 2 of 5
A Douglas-fir in a client's yard has needles that look speckled with white fluff, yellow spots and bent needles, and there are no galls. A blue spruce is next door. Best explanation?
No galls on Douglas-fir
  • NRCan: on Douglas-fir, white "woolly" flocculence marks the feeding site and needles are discoloured and distorted; feeding on secondary hosts causes distortion and eventual loss of needles, twigs or stems near the feeding site.
  • CSU: no galls on Douglas-fir; yellow spots and bent needles; trees appear speckled with cottony forms.
  • Penn State: heavily infested needles look covered in snow; possible discoloration, distortion and premature needle drop.
📋 Field Note: Okanagan

If you see the white fluff on a Douglas-fir, look at the nearby spruce. If the alternate host is close, expect galls there next spring.

Question 3 of 5
Which statement about hosts and life cycle is correct?
Primary vs secondary
  • NRCan: primary host (usually spruce) holds the sexual generation and the feeding there induces galls; the secondary host (here Douglas-fir) is a different genus where it reproduces asexually.
  • Both CSU and Penn State say the full cycle needs both spruce and Douglas-fir, though the insect can persist on Douglas-fir alone (Penn State: it may produce 5–6 generations a year there).
  • Duration: NRCan = two years when both hosts are present; CSU says it often takes a year. Say which source you're quoting.
Question 4 of 5
It's early November. A homeowner sees dozens of dry brown galls on her spruce and wants them "treated." What do you tell her?
Brown galls = empty galls
  • CSU: galls dry and brown from mid-July; the chambers open and winged forms emerge; the abandoned galls keep drying out. They usually don't cause serious harm; extremely heavy infestations may cause minor growth retardation and distortion.
  • NRCan: pruning out and destroying galls while they are still green and before adults emerge is effective in nurseries and on smaller ornamental trees. Penn State suggests June or July for removing green galls on a few small trees.
  • NRCan: severe infestations can reduce growth and the look of ornamental spruce; it's not considered a major pest in forests.
Question 5 of 5
In July a client wants spruce sprayed to kill the insects inside the fat green galls. What's the best response?
Timing and gall protection
  • CSU: once galls begin to form, insecticides usually are ineffective; best times are spring before new growth starts or fall when overwintering stages are back on the tree. Control is rarely needed to protect tree health, and infestations are highly cyclical.
  • NRCan: the gall and waxy coatings preclude most conventional pesticide treatments; insecticidal soaps broadcast on fundatrices in early spring can reduce populations on a small scale; registered products change year to year, so check Health Canada's Pesticide Product Information Database and consult a local tree care professional.
  • Penn State's US timing: spruce mid-Sept to early Oct, or spring before budbreak; Douglas-fir in April before budbreak or mid-Sept to early Oct. Use as a guide only; follow BC rules and the label.
