Question 1 of 5
ISA Domain: Tree Biology
What is "cavitation" in the context of tree water transport?

Breaking the Water Column: Cavitation Explained

Trees move water from soil to leaves entirely without a pump. The mechanism — cohesion-tension — relies on the tremendous tensile strength of water molecules bonded together in narrow xylem conduits. Transpiration at the leaf surface creates negative pressure (tension) that pulls water upward through this continuous column.

  • Water under tension is in a metastable state — it wants to vaporize, but cohesion holds it. When tension exceeds the air-seeding pressure of a pit membrane between adjacent conduits, air is drawn in from surrounding tissue
  • That bubble of air or water vapour — a cavitation event — immediately breaks the water column in that conduit. The conduit is now blocked by a gas-filled embolism
  • The embolized conduit loses its ability to conduct water. Hydraulic conductance in that pathway drops to zero
  • The tension threshold for cavitation varies widely by species — drought-tolerant species have lower (more negative) air-seeding pressures, meaning they resist cavitation longer
  • Conifer tracheids have bordered pits with torus-margo membranes that can actually aspirate (seal off) to isolate an embolism — a safety mechanism hardwood vessels largely lack
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Kelowna regularly sees July/August temperatures above 35°C with single-digit humidity. In those conditions, VPD (vapour pressure deficit) drives transpiration demand off the charts — trees are pulling water under massive tension. Ponderosa pine handles this aggressively (anisohydric strategy). When you see dead branches in the upper crown of a big pine in September, that's often the signature of cavitation that couldn't be reversed before the tree ran out of season. It's drought hydraulics written in dead wood.

Sources: ISA Certification Study Guide, 4th Ed., Ch. 3; Tyree & Sperry (1989) Plant Physiology; Brodribb & Cochard (2009) Plant Physiology

Question 2 of 5
ISA Domain: Tree Biology
Which plant hormone is the primary signal that triggers stomatal closure when a tree experiences drought stress?

ABA: The Drought Alarm Signal

Abscisic acid is often called the "stress hormone." When soil water deficit is detected, root cells synthesize ABA and load it into the xylem stream. It travels upward to the leaves, where it binds to receptors on guard cells — the paired cells flanking each stomate.

  • Guard cell response to ABA: ABA binding triggers a cascade: calcium ions are released, potassium and chloride ions pump out of guard cells, guard cells lose turgor, and the stomate closes. This is rapid — full closure can occur in minutes
  • Stomatal closure cuts transpiration and thus xylem tension — buying time before cavitation. But it also cuts CO₂ entry, shutting down photosynthesis
  • ABA also has non-stomatal effects: it promotes root growth toward water, stimulates accumulation of compatible solutes for osmotic adjustment, and at high concentrations induces dormancy responses
  • Root-to-shoot ABA signalling can occur even before leaf water potential drops — some trees "know" the soil is drying before they feel it in their leaves. This is called chemical signalling vs. hydraulic signalling
  • Gibberellins promote stomatal opening; cytokinins from roots also promote opening — so the balance of these signals relative to ABA determines stomatal behaviour under stress
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

When clients ask why their newly planted tree looks stressed even though they're watering, it's often an ABA problem. Transplanting severs most of the root system — suddenly the remaining roots can't supply water fast enough, ABA floods the system, stomata slam shut, and the tree goes into drought-like stress even in wet soil. This is transplant shock at the hormonal level. It's why watering frequency matters less than root zone integrity, and why proper planting depth and minimal root disturbance pays off for years afterward.

Sources: ISA Certification Study Guide, 4th Ed., Ch. 2 — Physiology; Taiz & Zeiger, Plant Physiology, 6th Ed.

Question 3 of 5
ISA Domain: Tree Biology
A tree species is classified as "isohydric." What does this mean for its drought response strategy?

Isohydric vs. Anisohydric: Two Ways to Handle a Drought

Tree species differ fundamentally in how conservative they are with water. Two broad strategies have been defined based on how leaf water potential (Ψ) changes during drought:

  • Isohydric species ("same water"): maintain leaf Ψ within a narrow range regardless of drought severity by closing stomata aggressively. They sacrifice carbon gain to protect their hydraulics. Example: some poplar species. Safe, but costly in carbon during prolonged drought.
  • Anisohydric species ("different water"): allow leaf Ψ to swing widely, keeping stomata open longer to fix carbon even as drought worsens. They accept higher cavitation risk in exchange for productivity. Examples: ponderosa pine, grapevines. This works until it doesn't — if drought is severe enough, embolism accumulates past the hydraulic safety margin.
  • Reality is a spectrum, not a binary. Most trees fall somewhere between the extremes, and individual trees of the same species may shift strategy with age, drought duration, or vapour pressure deficit
  • A species' Hydraulic Safety Margin (HSM) — the buffer between its minimum operating water potential and its P50 — reflects how much risk it builds in. Narrow HSM = living close to the edge
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Ponderosa pine is the Okanagan's classic anisohydric species. Through late July and August, ponderosa lets its water potential drop far lower than Douglas-fir would tolerate — it's gambling on rain. In a normal year, that works; the monsoon pulse in August gives it just enough relief. In extended drought years like those the Interior has seen since 2017, that gamble starts to fail. When you see ponderosa with dead tops and pitch tubes mid-crown, you're looking at an anisohydric strategy that ran out of margin.

Sources: ISA Certification Study Guide; Tardieu & Simonneau (1998) J. Experimental Botany; McDowell et al. (2008) New Phytologist

Question 4 of 5
ISA Domain: Tree Biology
In a hydraulic vulnerability curve, the term P50 refers to:

Hydraulic Vulnerability Curves: Reading a Tree's Tolerance

A hydraulic vulnerability curve plots the percentage loss of hydraulic conductance (PLC) against xylem water potential (Ψ, measured in megapascals, MPa). It is the definitive way to characterize how drought-resistant a species' xylem is.

  • P50: The Ψ at which 50% of xylem conductance is lost. Species with more negative P50 values (e.g., −5 MPa vs. −2 MPa) can withstand greater drought tension before losing half their conductance
  • P88: The Ψ at 88% conductance loss — often used as a proxy for the lethal hydraulic threshold, where recovery becomes unlikely without severe dieback
  • P12: The point at which embolism begins — the stomata of most species close around or before P12 in isohydric species, providing a safety buffer
  • The Hydraulic Safety Margin (HSM) is the gap between P50 and the minimum Ψ a species typically operates at in the field. A narrow HSM (e.g., 0.5 MPa) means little buffer; a wide one (e.g., 2+ MPa) means much more
  • Measuring P50 requires laboratory bench dehydration and hydraulic conductance testing — it's research-level data, but published values exist for most commercially important tree species
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Published P50 values put ponderosa pine around −3 to −3.5 MPa and interior Douglas-fir around −4 MPa — both are reasonably drought tolerant by design. Norway maple (a common boulevard tree in Kelowna) has a P50 of around −2.5 MPa and a much narrower hydraulic safety margin under drought — which is one reason Norway maples planted in tight boulevard strips with compacted soils decline faster than people expect. When a client has a stressed maple next to a paved boulevard, the hydraulic vulnerability curve is part of the story.

Sources: Choat et al. (2012) Nature — global vulnerability curves; Anderegg et al. (2016) PNAS; ISA Certification Study Guide, Ch. 3

Question 5 of 5
ISA Domain: Tree Biology · Tree Risk Assessment
When cumulative xylem embolism in a tree exceeds its hydraulic safety margin during a prolonged drought, what is the most likely outcome without rainfall or intervention?

Hydraulic Failure: When the System Collapses

Hydraulic failure is a cascade, not a single event. Embolism accumulates as drought worsens, each blocked conduit reducing the tree's capacity to supply water to leaves. Two reinforcing processes drive mortality:

  • Hydraulic failure pathway: As conductance drops below the threshold needed to supply transpiring leaves, leaves desiccate and die. Crown dieback follows a predictable pattern — small terminal branches first, then larger limbs, then whole scaffold branches. Tip dieback is often the first visible sign
  • Carbon starvation pathway: Stomatal closure during drought halts photosynthesis. Non-structural carbohydrate (NSC) reserves are drawn down to support respiration. Over months to a full season, NSC depletion compromises everything that requires energy — defence against pathogens, wound response, root growth, new xylem production
  • The two pathways interact: Most drought-induced mortality involves both. Low NSC means the tree can't grow new xylem to replace embolized vessels; poor hydraulics mean weak photosynthesis means low NSC — it's a death spiral
  • Embolism can be reversed under some conditions — positive root pressure in spring (particularly in ring-porous hardwoods like oaks), or osmotically driven refilling — but severe, widespread embolism beyond P88 is generally irreversible without losing the affected tissue
  • Secondary opportunists: Bark beetles, Ips species, Armillaria, and other pathogens key in on hydraulically stressed trees. The cambium relies on water-saturated resin flow to exclude borers — when that fails, trees become targets
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

The BC Interior drought of 2021 (the year of Lytton) was a turning point for drought-induced mortality across the Okanagan. Trees that survived the summer started dying in 2022 and 2023 — the delayed mortality signature of NSC depletion paired with opportunistic pathogens. When you're called to assess a tree that "just started dying," ask when the last significant drought was. Trees can coast on stored reserves for 1–2 seasons post-stress before the bill comes due. Drought mortality is often a cold case, not a fresh one.

Sources: McDowell et al. (2008) New Phytologist — carbon starvation vs. hydraulic failure framework; ISA Certification Study Guide, 4th Ed.; Allen et al. (2010) Forest Ecology & Management
