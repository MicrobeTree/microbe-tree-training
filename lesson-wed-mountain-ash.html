Mountain-Ash (Sorbus): ID, Kelowna Use, Problems & Care

It's not an ash, it's a rose. Learn to tell European from native, know why Okanagan mountain-ash fail early, and know what to tell the client.

🍒 Fast facts

Sorbus, rose family (Rosaceae)
Alternate, pinnately compound, toothed leaflets
Flat-topped white clusters → orange-red pomes
True ash (Fraxinus) has opposite leaves
Fire blight · sunscald-canker · borers
~120 city trees, mostly showy mountain-ash

Compact, pretty, loaded with berries, and short-lived on hot, dry sites. Mountain-ash is a strata and boulevard favourite that keeps us busy. This lesson covers ID, local use, its problems and how we care for it. For when and how to cut fire blight out, see Thursday's Fire Blight & Pruning Timing lesson. It isn't repeated here.

Question 1 of 5
Mid-winter, no leaves. Which bud tells you you're looking at European mountain-ash (Sorbus aucuparia)?
Fuzzy grey buds = European

Using E-Flora BC's key and NRCan's showy mountain-ash factsheet:

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

The natives are mostly mountain shrubs you'll see on hikes and at higher-elevation properties. The single-stem "mountain-ash" trees in town are planted, either European or showy. E-Flora notes European mountain-ash is an emerging invasive in the Vancouver region.

Question 2 of 5
A client calls their tree an "ash" and is worried about emerald ash borer talk. You see alternate, compound leaves and orange berries. What do you tell them?
Name ≠ family
  • E-Flora: Sorbus leaves are alternate and pinnately compound. USU: green ash (Fraxinus pennsylvanica, olive family) has opposite, pinnately compound leaves. Leaf arrangement settles it in seconds, and it's an ISA ID staple.
  • Being in the rose family puts mountain-ash in the same disease club as apple, pear, crabapple and hawthorn. The PNW Plant Disease Handbook rates European and American mountain-ash as very susceptible to fire blight, and calls it a problem mostly east of the Cascades when bloom is warm. That's us.
📋 Field Note — Microbe Tree Crew

For blight timing, cut distances and tool sanitation, use the Fire Blight & Pruning Timing lesson. Here, just recognize the symptoms: blackened, "scorched" shoot tips, shepherd's crooks and purplish cankers.

Question 3 of 5
According to the City of Kelowna's open tree inventory, which Sorbus makes up most of the city-maintained mountain-ash?
Kelowna's numbers

We queried the City of Kelowna's open Tree Inventory on Oct 15, 2026: about 24,600 city-maintained trees, of which 120 are Sorbus: 112 showy mountain-ash (S. decora) and 8 European (S. aucuparia). Median trunk diameter (DBH) is about 15 cm, so they're mostly small trees.

  • Showy mountain-ash is native to eastern Canada, not BC (VASCAN). The PNW Handbook names S. americana 'Decorah' as a resistant choice, so ask about the cultivar as well as the species.
  • That's the City of Kelowna's own trees. West Kelowna, strata and private yards aren't in that dataset, so expect more European mountain-ash out there.
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

When a client wants a replacement, don't plant a blight-susceptible tree on a hot, south-facing strata slope out of habit. Talk through species and cultivar choice.

Question 4 of 5
A 10-year-old mountain-ash on a west-facing boulevard has browning on the southwest side only, and there's an old vertical bark crack on that side of the trunk. Most likely culprit?
Thin bark + southwest sun
  • Sunscald (Wisconsin Extension A2517): mountain-ash is thin-barked. In late winter or very early spring, sun heats the south- and west-facing bark well above air temperature. At sunset it drops suddenly, often leaving a sharp vertical crack. Fungi move into the wound and slowly kill the cambium around the trunk. Leaves on the affected side yellow or brown, often on the southwest side. Wisconsin calls it probably the most common cause of early death in mountain-ash there, and notes it's often confused with fire blight.
  • Borers follow damage: UMass lists mountain-ash among hosts of flatheaded appletree borer. CSU says flatheaded borers favour damaged and drought-stressed trees and lay eggs in bark cracks on sun-exposed trunks. Trees are most susceptible 2–3 years after transplanting, and adults leave D-shaped exit holes. The closely related Pacific flatheaded borer (Chrysobothris mali) is the more common one west of the Rockies.

Check: peel back a little bark at the edge of the crack. Brown inner bark where it should be green means dead cambium. Look for D-shaped holes, frass and bark splitting over galleries.

Question 5 of 5
Which care plan best fits a young European mountain-ash on a hot Okanagan strata slope?
Protect the bark, don't push soft growth
  • Don't over-prune. Even bare branches shade the trunk. Where appropriate, leave low branches to reduce stem exposure (Wisconsin).
  • Winter protection for young trees: wrap the bark before winter with a suitable material to shade it (Wisconsin). Microbe practice: take wraps off in spring so they don't hide cracks or borer signs.
  • Avoid wounds. Mower and trimmer damage starts the same canker cycle as sunscald (Wisconsin). The PNW Handbook also says avoid wounding plants.
  • Moderate nitrogen to limit the vigorous shoots that fire blight targets, and don't combine routine pruning with blight removal (PNW Handbook).
  • Water through drought. Drought-stressed trees are more prone to flatheaded borers (CSU).
  • Timing: see the Fire Blight & Pruning Timing lesson for why the dormant season is when cankers come out.
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Strata slopes facing south or west, reflected heat off siding and rock, and irrigation that stops in September: that's the high-risk mountain-ash site. Mention it in the client report, along with what to watch for.
