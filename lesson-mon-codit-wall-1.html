CODIT Part 1: Wall 1, the Weakest Wall

How trees try to stop decay running up and down the stem, why they mostly fail, and what that means when you climb, rig or fell around old cuts.

🌳 CODIT in two lines

  • CODIT = Compartmentalization Of Decay In Trees, a model Dr. Alex Shigo (USDA Forest Service) developed in the 1970s. Trees don't heal wounds; they wall them off and grow new wood over them.
  • The model has four walls. Walls 1–3 work in the wood that was there when the tree was wounded; Wall 4 (the barrier zone) is laid down by the cambium afterward.
📅 Monday series: one wall per week. Today: Wall 1. Coming up on the next Mondays: Wall 2 (inward), Wall 3 (around the stem), Wall 4 (the barrier zone).

🧱 Wall 1: plugging the pipes

  • Job: resists decay spreading up and down (vertically, along the grain) from a wound.
  • How: the tree plugs its water-conducting xylem above and below the wound with tyloses, gums and resins (Penn State Extension; Bartlett). Shigo describes "many variations on the theme of vessel plugging and pit closure".
  • Strength: it's the weakest of the four walls. Shigo's reason: the growing tree still has to move water vertically, so it can't seal those channels off tightly. Penn State: it's hard to plug the xylem tight enough to stop fungi spreading along the branch or trunk.
  • Result: Bartlett: Wall 1 lets decay organisms spread farthest in a vertical direction. Decay columns are typically long and narrow.

✂️ Why it matters for pruning (dormant season is here)

  • Cut just outside the branch collar. USDA Forest Service (Smith 2006): the base of a branch has a branch protection zone with built-in chemicals that resist infection. A flush cut removes some or all of that protection wood, makes a bigger wound and injures the stem.
  • No long stubs. Same source: long stubs interfere with wound closure and give decay organisms a food base.
  • Topping is the worst case for Wall 1. Stems don't have pre-formed boundaries along their length the way branch bases do. Boundaries do form against axial spread, but the open cell structure and less-responsive heartwood make extensive spread of decay likely (Smith 2006).
  • Skip the wound paint. Penn State: sealing with tar or oil-based paint doesn't prevent or stop decay, and oil-based sealants inhibit closure. Smith 2006: compartmentalization isn't aided by wound dressings.
⚠️ Hazards of Wall 1: decay runs longBecause Wall 1 is the weakest, the rot behind an old wound can run a long way above and below what you can see from the outside. Treat old topping cuts, flush cuts and big stubs as the top or bottom of a hidden column, not as a small local spot.

🧗 Climbing & tie-in points

  • USDA Forest Service National Tree Climbing Guide: after topping, "the stub inevitably decays, providing very poor support for new epicormic branches". Inspect all branch unions at or below your tie-in point before you commit to them.
  • TCIA: in a tree with noticeable decay, ascend in shorter steps (branch by branch) rather than throwlining high. Your climbing line puts bending loads on the stem well below the TIP, so check for decay well below the TIP too.

🪢 Rigging off a previously cut leader

  • TCIA: a rigged tree is a crane. Check the trunk and unions below the rigging point, and look for decay columns. Rigging adds dynamic load on top of the wood's own weight.
  • A regrown leader on an old topping cut is sitting on a stub that has been decaying. Don't set a block or redirect there without checking the wood beneath it.

🪓 Felling & hinge wood

  • If you suspect rot, Husqvarna's felling guide says to make an inspection bore in the middle of the hinge, below the felling cut; check for discoloured, soft wood; and make sure the hinge is cut in healthy wood, by adjusting the notch depth or cutting higher where the wood is usually healthier. If the hinge is weak, fell in the natural direction or secure with ropes.
  • TCIA's felling plan: make a sounding mallet routine to listen for hollows.
  • A decay column that runs down from an old wound can reach your hinge height even when the bark at the stump looks fine. Rotten hinge wood = no steering.
📋 Field Note: the topped tree you've all seen

A backyard tree in the Okanagan that was topped years ago now has a ring of upright sprouts around the old cut. From the ground the stub looks sound and the new leaders look healthy. But that cut has had years for decay to run down the old leader, and those sprouts are attached to a decaying stub. Don't tie in to them or rig off them until you've checked the wood: sound it, look for soft or discoloured wood, conks or cracks, and pick a TIP or rigging point below the decay or in another stem. When you make the cuts this season, cut at the collar and don't leave new stubs. You're setting up the next crew's tree.

Question 1 of 5
Which direction does CODIT Wall 1 resist decay spreading?
Wall 1 = vertical
  • Shigo (1984): wall 1 resists vertical spread, wall 2 inward, wall 3 lateral (tangential). Wall 4 separates wood present at wounding from new wood.
  • Penn State: Wall 1 limits spread up and down the length of the branch or trunk.
Question 2 of 5
How does a tree build Wall 1?
Plugging the vertical pipes
  • Penn State: the tree plugs vascular channels (water-conducting xylem) with tyloses or gums next to the wound.
  • Bartlett: formed in the vertical vascular system by gums, resins, tyloses, etc.
  • The cambium option describes Wall 4; ray cells are Wall 3. Those are coming up on later Mondays.
Question 3 of 5
Why is Wall 1 the weakest wall?
The plumbing has to keep working
  • Shigo (1984): "Wall 1 is the weakest, since the growing tree must maintain vertical transport."
  • Penn State: it's hard to plug the xylem tight enough to stop fungi spreading along the branch or trunk.
  • Bartlett: that's why decay spreads farthest vertically.

Question 4 of 5
You're asked to climb a maple that was topped years ago. Strong sprouts have grown up around the old cut and the stub looks sound from the ground. What's the right call?
Old cut = top of a hidden column
  • USDA National Tree Climbing Guide: after topping, the stub inevitably decays, giving very poor support to the new epicormic branches. Inspect all branch unions at or below your tie-in point.
  • TCIA: in a decayed tree, ascend in shorter steps and assess decay well below the TIP, because your line bends the stem below it.
  • Smith 2006 (USDA FS): after topping, extensive spread of decay is likely. That's the weakness of Wall 1.
Question 5 of 5
You're about to make the final cut on an old branch. Which cut gives the tree's defences the best chance?
Protect the protection zone
  • Smith 2006: a flush cut removes some or all of the branch protection zone, makes a larger wound and injures the stem. Long stubs interfere with closure and feed decay organisms.
  • Penn State: tar or oil-based sealants don't prevent or stop decay and oil-based ones inhibit wound closure.
