Mechanical Advantage — The Physics

Mechanical Advantage (MA) is the ratio of the output force (load) to the input force (effort) in a system. In arborist work, MA systems use ropes and pulleys to allow one or two people to move loads that would otherwise require far more force — a suspended log, a stuck load in a rigging line, or a person in a rope rescue.

TMA = Output Force / Input Force TMA = Number of rope segments supporting the load AMA = TMA × efficiency factor (typically 0.85–0.90 per pulley)

TMA — Theoretical Mechanical Advantage

TMA is the simple rope-counting calculation — count the number of rope segments running to (or from) the moving block or load. This gives the ideal MA in a frictionless system.

  • 1:1 system — 1 rope segment at the load; no MA. A single pulley redirects force but doesn't multiply it. 100 kg load requires 100 kg pull
  • 2:1 system — 2 rope segments support the load (one fixed, one running). 100 kg load requires 50 kg pull (TMA = 2)
  • 3:1 system — 3 rope segments. 100 kg load requires approximately 33 kg pull (TMA = 3)
  • 5:1 system — 5 rope segments. 100 kg load requires approximately 20 kg pull (TMA = 5). Common compound MA configuration

The Rope Counting Rule

To find TMA quickly: draw a line at the moving block (or load). Count the number of ropes crossing that line. That number is the TMA.

Example — 3:1 Z-rig: Anchor — [fixed pulley] — rope — [moving pulley] — rope — [fixed pulley] — haul Three ropes cross the "load line" → TMA = 3

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

MA systems are not just for rescue — in day-to-day arborist work, they come up whenever a rigging line is jammed, a log section hangs at a bad angle, or you need to reposition heavy material in a confined yard. The Z-rig is the fastest to build in the field. Every climber and every ground crew person should be able to rig a 3:1 from a pulley and a rope in under 2 minutes. If you can't do that, practice it on the ground before you need it 60 feet up a ponderosa at 3 PM.

Z-Rig & C-Rig — The Two Standard Field Configurations

3:1 Z-Rig — Most Common Arborist MA System

The Z-rig gets its name from the Z-shape the rope makes when viewed from the side. It provides a 3:1 TMA with minimal hardware — just two pulleys and a progress capture device. It is the standard field MA for stuck loads, controlled lowering assistance, and rope rescue.

Building a Z-rig (3:1):

  • Step 1: Attach a pulley to your anchor point (high rigging point, trunk, or anchor sling). This is the fixed redirect pulley
  • Step 2: Run your haul line through the fixed pulley — it returns toward the load
  • Step 3: Attach a second (moving) pulley to the load or to the load-side rope using a prusik or bowline loop. This pulley travels with the load
  • Step 4: Run the rope back through the fixed redirect pulley (or to the haulers directly). The haul direction is now away from the load, typically back toward the anchor
  • Step 5: Install a progress capture device — a prusik on the line between the load and the fixed pulley — to hold progress when the haulers reset their grip
  • Result: Three rope segments support the load = TMA 3

C-Rig (Reeve System) — 2:1 with Specific Geometry

The C-rig (or "reeve system") is a 2:1 MA where the rope is anchored at the load, runs up through a fixed pulley, and returns to the hauler. The C-shape is formed by the anchor-to-load-to-pulley-to-hauler geometry.

  • TMA = 2; simpler than Z-rig but half the MA
  • Advantage: the hauler can pull in the same direction as the load travel (load goes up, haul direction is down or away — natural body mechanics)
  • Common in single-rope rescue where a 2:1 is sufficient and simplicity matters
  • The moving element is the load itself (the rope end anchors to the load, then routes through a fixed block above)

Converting a 3:1 to 6:1 — Compound MA

A compound MA system uses one MA system to pull on another:

  • Attach the haul line of a 3:1 Z-rig to the moving block of a 2:1 system → compound 3×2 = 6:1 TMA
  • More practically: 3:1 Z-rig on the haul line of another 3:1 = 9:1 TMA — rarely needed but possible with available hardware
  • Each pulley added in a compound system also adds friction — AMA falls further below TMA
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

In tight residential yards in West Kelowna, space for hauling is often the limiting factor. A 3:1 requires 3 m of haul line for every 1 m the log moves. In a 4 m wide driveway, you'll get through one or two hauls before you run out of room and need to reset the progress capture. Build that into your rigging plan — confirm the haul distance before you cut. Resetting a progress capture while managing a suspended log section is a three-handed job. Know who is doing what before any wood starts moving.

Friction & Actual Mechanical Advantage (AMA)

Every pulley in a system introduces friction. Even a high-quality ball-bearing pulley absorbs some energy as heat, reducing the force transmitted to the load. This gap between TMA and AMA is a critical safety consideration — your system is never as efficient as the numbers suggest.

Efficiency Per Pulley

Pulley TypeApproximate EfficiencyTypical Use
High-quality ball-bearing arborist pulley (Petzl, ISC, DMM)90–95%Rigging, MA systems
Standard aluminum side-plate pulley85–90%General rigging
Carabiner acting as pulley (rope running through gate)50–65%Emergency only — very inefficient
Friction hitch (prusik, VT) as progress captureAdds load; reduces efficiency by ~10–15%Progress capture; not a pulley
AMA calculation example — 3:1 Z-rig with ball-bearing pulleys: TMA = 3 Efficiency per pulley ≈ 0.90 (90%) 2 pulleys in system AMA ≈ 3 × (0.90 × 0.90) = 3 × 0.81 = ~2.4 A "3:1" system with realistic friction delivers about 2.4:1 actual MA.

Practical Implications

  • A 3:1 system with two standard pulleys realistically delivers about 2.4:1 to 2.7:1 depending on pulley quality, rope stiffness, and bend angles
  • A 5:1 compound system with 4 pulleys and friction hitches may deliver only 3:1 to 3.5:1 AMA despite the theoretical 5:1
  • Rope stiffness matters — stiff 13 mm rigging line loses more energy in bends than supple 11 mm climbing rope
  • Sharp bend angles (rope bent tightly around small-diameter carabiners) dramatically increase friction — minimize tight bends, maximize pulley sheave diameter relative to rope diameter
  • Design rule: When calculating required MA for a task, use AMA in your force calculations, not TMA. Add a safety margin — design to AMA with a 50% buffer

Calculating Required MA for a Known Load

Required haul force = Load weight / AMA Example: 150 kg log section, 3:1 Z-rig (AMA ~2.5) Required haul force = 150 / 2.5 = 60 kg per hauler Two-person haul team = 120 kg pulling force — sufficient. One person alone = 60 kg — may be marginal. Add a 5:1 or get a second hauler.
⚠ Never exceed hardware WLL. MA systems multiply force at the anchor and rigging points, not just at the load. A 3:1 system pulling with 60 kg of force generates 180 kg of load on the anchor pulley connection. Always check the WLL (Working Load Limit) of every component — carabiners, pulleys, slings, and rigging rings — against the calculated anchor load, not the haul load.

Applications in Arborist Work

1. Rescuing a Stuck Rigging Line

A common fieldwork scenario: a cut log is hung up on a crotch or co-dominant stem and won't come free with ground pull alone. A 3:1 Z-rig rigged from a separate anchor point (climbing line, pre-installed redirect) provides enough force to break it free without exceeding equipment limits.

  • Anchor the fixed block high and away from the stuck load — maintain a favorable load angle
  • Attach the moving block to the hung rigging line via a bowline or wrap-and-cinch
  • Install progress capture before hauling
  • Communicate clearly — ground crew and climber must coordinate before force is applied to a stuck load. Sudden release can cause violent swing

2. Controlled Ground-Level Positioning of Heavy Logs

Once a log section is on the ground, a 2:1 or 3:1 can be used to roll, reposition, or drag it clear of the work zone — reducing back injury risk from direct lifting of heavy wood.

  • Anchor redirect to a nearby tree, vehicle hitch, or ground anchor stake
  • Loop rigging line around the log (timber hitch or loop with shackle) — pull from a safe angle
  • Keep all crew out of the load's potential travel path before hauling

3. SRS Rope Rescue — Rope Rescue for a Hanging Climber

Per ANSI Z133:2017 §14 and ISA emergency response guidelines, every arborist crew must be capable of raising or lowering a suspended incapacitated climber. A 3:1 system rigged from the aerial attachment point can raise a person for extrication from a split crotch or tangled equipment situation.

  • All crew must know how to build a 3:1 from available hardware before they need it under stress
  • Progress capture is mandatory — no system should rely on crew holding tension continuously
  • Coordinate with the victim when possible — they may be able to assist or redirect the haul rope
  • Practice this drill regularly. Per ANSI Z133 §4.2, emergency procedures shall be rehearsed at intervals sufficient to maintain proficiency

4. Rigging Assistance on Difficult Directional Cuts

When a large section needs to be held against its natural fall direction and lowered in a controlled path, a 2:1 rigging assist (rigging line through a block, back to the groundie) doubles the holding power of the ground crew — critical when a single person is managing a 300–400 kg log section.

⚠ Every person on the haul team must know the plan. Who is hauling, who is tending the progress capture, who is watching the load and the anchor, and who is communicating with the climber (if applicable) — all must be established before force is applied. Per ANSI Z133, a crew member shall be designated to maintain communication with the climber during any rigging operation involving a load in the aerial.
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

The most underused MA skill on the job site is the pre-plan. Before any large piece comes out of a tree, the ground crew should be able to answer: what does this piece weigh (roughly)? What's our rigging configuration? What MA do we have? If the section hangs up, what's the rescue plan? On a well-run crew, this takes 60 seconds at the tailgate. On a poorly-run crew, these decisions get made in real time while a 200 kg log is suspended over a fence. Know your system before the cut. Every time.
