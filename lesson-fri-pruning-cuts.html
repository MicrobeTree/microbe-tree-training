Pruning Cuts

Branch collar, branch bark ridge, the 3-cut method, drop crotch — getting every cut right the first time

📋 ANSI A300 Part 1 (2017) — The Standard

"Pruning cuts shall be made outside the branch bark ridge and branch collar without leaving a stub or making a flush cut." This is the governing standard for every pruning cut on every job. Know it cold.

🔍 Branch Attachment Anatomy

Diagram — Branch Collar and Branch Bark Ridge

The Branch Collar

What it is

The branch collar is a zone of trunk tissue that wraps around the base of every branch. It is NOT branch tissue — it is stem tissue, chemically and anatomically distinct from the branch wood itself. Alex Shigo's CODIT research established that the collar contains the primary compartmentalization boundary (Wall 4) for the stem.

  • Contains highly reactive wound-response tissue
  • When a branch dies naturally, the collar grows over the base and seals the wound from the stem side
  • If you cut through the collar (flush cut), you destroy this response capacity entirely — the stem is now directly exposed
  • On large branches, the collar may be quite pronounced and visible as a distinct swelling ring at the branch base
  • On small branches with narrow angles, the collar is less distinct but still present

The Branch Bark Ridge (BBR)

Your cut angle guide

The BBR is a ridge of bark in the upper crotch angle where the branch meets the stem. It is your angle reference for making the correct cut.

  • Mirror the angle of the BBR on the upper side of the branch when setting your saw angle
  • This gives you a cut that is outside the collar on the bottom and at the right angle on the top
  • On branches with tight crotch angles, the BBR is steep — cut angle is steeper
  • On wide-angled branches, the BBR is nearly flat — cut angle is more horizontal
  • When the BBR is indistinct (decay, previous damage), look for the collar swelling at the base to guide cut placement
  • Never make the cut angle steeper than necessary — excessive angle increases wound surface area
⚠️ Why Flush Cuts Kill Trees (Or Kill the Stem)

The Flush Cut Problem — Shigo's CODIT in Action

Before Shigo's research in the 1970s–80s, the prevailing wisdom was to cut flush with the stem and then apply wound dressing — get it smooth, seal it, let it "heal over." This was wrong on every count, and Shigo proved it with dissection of thousands of trees.

  • Flush cut removes the collar: The collar tissue is where Walls 3 and 4 of CODIT originate in relation to the branch wound. Remove it and you've eliminated the primary barrier between the branch stub decay and the stem interior
  • Massively increases wound size: A flush cut exposes a wound that spans the full diameter of the branch plus the collar tissue. A correctly placed cut outside the collar exposes only the branch cross-section, which is smaller
  • Decay enters the stem: Without a collar, decay organisms that colonize the stub wound have direct access to the stem wood. This creates stem decay that can extend longitudinally — up and down the trunk — far beyond the original wound
  • Wound closure is impaired: The collar is what drives wound closure (callus formation). Remove it and closure is dramatically slower — sometimes never complete on large wounds
  • Wound dressings don't help: Shigo's research definitively showed wound dressings (tar, paint, petroleum products) do not improve wound closure rates, do not prevent decay, and may actually impede closure by trapping moisture
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

You will encounter flush-cut trees on virtually every residential property job. Decades of bad arborist practice — and worse, homeowner pruning — left a legacy of flush cuts on fruit trees, maples, and ornamentals throughout the Okanagan. When you see a large depression in a trunk where a branch was obviously removed flush, probe it with a screwdriver. If you find soft, punky wood, that's stem decay originating from a flush cut. Document it in the assessment: "Evidence of previous flush cuts resulting in stem decay — recommend formal risk assessment per TRAQ methodology before determining retention." You can't fix past flush cuts, but you can quantify the damage and communicate the risk clearly.

🪚 The 3-Cut Method — Heavy Limb Removal

Diagram — 3-Cut Sequence for Branch Over 5 cm Diameter

1

Cut 1 — The Undercut

Start approximately 25–40 cm from the final cut position, on the underside of the branch. Cut upward through roughly one-third of the branch diameter. Do not cut more than half through — the saw can bind and the branch can pinch the bar.

Purpose: Creates a bark crack-stopper. When the limb's weight causes it to drop during Cut 2, the tear in the bark travels down and arrests at the undercut rather than stripping back to the trunk.

2

Cut 2 — The Top Cut

Move 2–3 cm further from the trunk than your undercut. Cut downward from the top until the branch falls free. The bark strip, if any, runs from Cut 2 downward and stops cleanly at the undercut notch.

Escape route: Know where the limb is going before you start Cut 2. Position yourself on the safe side. Per ANSI Z133 Section 9.1, establish your escape route before beginning any felling or limb-dropping operation — this principle applies to lowering ops too.

3

Cut 3 — The Final Cut

With the limb's weight removed, make the final cut outside the branch bark ridge and branch collar per ANSI A300 Part 1. Set your saw angle to mirror the BBR. On the bottom side, clear the collar. No stub remaining.

Check your work: The finished cut should be clean, slightly oval on large branches, with no stub and no crushing of collar tissue. The BBR should be intact and undamaged.

✂️ Drop Crotch Reduction Cuts

Diagram — Drop Crotch vs Heading Cut

Drop Crotch — The Rule

Per ANSI A300 Part 1, a reduction cut must be made to a lateral branch that is at least one-third (1/3) the diameter of the limb being removed at the point of the cut. This ensures the remaining lateral has enough capacity to assume the water-conducting and structural role of the removed portion.

  • The lateral must be growing in a direction that maintains the intended crown form
  • Cut is made just outside the collar/bark ridge of the lateral, just as a standard removal cut
  • Result: the crown is reduced while maintaining a living growing tip — no stub, no stump
  • A lateral less than 1/3 the diameter may not be able to sustain the flow of water and nutrients previously moving through the main limb — it may die back

Heading Cuts — What Goes Wrong

A heading cut removes a branch or leader back to a stub with no adequate lateral to take over. Even when small laterals are present below the cut, if they are too small, the stub dies back and becomes an entry point for decay.

  • Creates flush or near-flush stubs that cannot callus over quickly — decay organisms enter
  • Triggers vigorous epicormic sprouting below the cut — watersprouts are structurally weak, poorly attached, and increase re-pruning frequency
  • Over time: topping (the worst form of heading cut) creates multiple entry points, massive crown dieback, and structurally compromised resprout growth
  • ISA strongly opposes topping as contrary to ANSI A300 Part 1 and ISA BMP for Pruning
  • Topping also significantly reduces the tree's aesthetic and monetary value — a relevant client conversation point
✅ The Non-Negotiables
✓ Always

Use the 3-cut method for any branch where weight could cause bark stripping. Make all final cuts outside the BBR and collar per ANSI A300 Part 1. Size up the collar before cutting — an some species (fruit trees, apples, older maples) the collar is very pronounced. Angle the cut to mirror the BBR on the upper side. Keep saw chains sharp — a dull chain tears, crushes, and damages the collar tissue you're trying to protect.

✗ Never

Never make a flush cut — even if the client requests it. "Flush cuts kill trees" is a conversation worth having before the work, not after. Never apply wound paint or tree seal to a properly made cut — it offers no benefit and can trap moisture. Never make a reduction cut to a lateral less than 1/3 the diameter of the limb being removed. Never leave a stub — a stub is a future decay entry point and a future hazard limb. If you can't make a proper collar cut, get a different angle or a different saw position — get a compromise cut.

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

The ISA CA exam will test pruning cut knowledge directly — it is in the Pruning domain (15% of the exam, the second-largest domain). But more importantly, you will be evaluated on your pruning cuts by every client who watches you work and every arborist who inspects the trees afterward. A correctly made collar cut on a 15 cm branch heals within 3–7 years on a healthy tree in the Okanagan climate. A flush cut on the same branch may leave a permanently exposed wound that decays into the stem over decades. The difference is about 5 seconds of correct positioning before you pull the trigger. Get it right every time.

Summary Table
