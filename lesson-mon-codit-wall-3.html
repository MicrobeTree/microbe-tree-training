How rays keep decay from wrapping around the stem, what happens when they lose, and how to judge the shell before you climb or cut.

🧱 Wall 3: the ray-cell wall

  • Built from rays. Bartlett: Wall 3 is composed of ray cells. Penn State: it forms in living ray cells, using active defence chemicals to stop the circular spread of decay. Mississippi State: radial xylem rays prevent the horizontal progression of symptoms.
  • Rays run like spokes from the pith out to the bark, so they make natural side walls that keep a decay wedge from widening around the stem.
  • Strongest of the first three. Shigo (1984) calls Wall 3 the strongest wall in part 1 of the model (Walls 1–3). Bartlett: the strongest wall present at the time of wounding.
  • Built in before the injury. Shigo: Walls 1, 2 and 3 exist before injury and infection. Wall 4 forms after.

⚖️ The direction words conflict between sources. Shigo says Wall 2 resists inward (radial) spread and Wall 3 resists lateral (tangential) spread. Bartlett flips the labels: it calls Wall 2 the tangential wall and says Wall 3 blocks decay "in a radial direction (around the stem)". Don't memorise the jargon. Memorise the picture: Wall 3 = ray cells = around the stem = strongest of the first three.
⚠️ Hazards of Wall 3: the column widens into a hollowShigo: hollows form as Wall 3 fails over time and the boundaries open like a fan. A wedge of decay held between rays slowly becomes a column that wraps around the stem. What's left is a shell of sound wood outside the hollow. The USDA climbing guide says trees usually decay from the inside out while new sound wood keeps being added outside, so the bark can look fine over a thin shell.

🔍 What that means at work

  • Early decay is the sneaky part. Forestpathology.org: the most rapid strength loss can happen in the early stages, when decay is barely visible. Don't treat only soft, punky wood as a problem.
  • Sounding. A very hollow stem gives a "bass drum" sound when you strike it with a mallet or the back of a hatchet. Decay without a big void is much harder to hear, and the sound varies between species.
  • Drilling. A cordless drill with a long thin bit lets you feel the wood's hardness. You can drill several radii quickly to map the column. An increment borer measures sound-wood thickness fairly accurately, but leaves a bigger hole.
  • The shell rule of thumb (forestpathology.org): at least 1/3 of the radius should be sound shell. Get the radius from DBH ÷ 2, not from the core. That's a hazard-rating guide, not a climbing or felling guarantee.
  • Climbing. USDA climbing guide: a sound shell may be relatively safe, but it depends on the ratio of sound to decayed wood. If you have doubts, avoid it. TCIA: in decayed trees, check well below the tie-in point.
  • WorkSafeBC 26.12.0.2(2)(a): before climbing, a qualified arborist must determine that each tree to be climbed can withstand the loads of the climbing work. Under 26.12.0.3, the tree-climbing plan has to consider recurring failure patterns for the species, such as rot.
📋 Field Note: judging the shell before you cut

An old cottonwood by a creek sounds like a drum at chest height and has an open cavity on one side. You measure 60 cm DBH, so the radius is 30 cm and the 1/3 guide asks for at least 10 cm of sound shell. Drill several radii, not just one, because Wall 3 lets the column spread unevenly around the stem. Plan on the thinnest reading. If you're felling, Husqvarna says to make an inspection bore in the hinge area below the felling cut, check for discoloured or soft wood, and keep the hinge in healthy wood and as long as possible. Hollow trees get the same method. Fell in the natural direction or secure with ropes. Thin shell, cavity opening or doubt: stop and talk to Anton before anyone climbs or cuts.

Question 4 of 5
According to Shigo, how do hollows form?
The fan opens
  • Shigo (1984): hollows form as wall 3, the strongest wall in part 1, fails over time and the boundaries open like a fan.

Question 5 of 5
A hollow stem is 60 cm DBH. Using forestpathology.org's rating rule of thumb, what's the minimum sound shell, and how should you use that number?
Radius ÷ 3, then judgment
  • Forestpathology.org: at least 1/3 of the radius left sound. Use DBH/2 for the radius. 60 ÷ 2 = 30 cm, and 30 ÷ 3 = 10 cm.
  • Drill several radii to picture the column. USDA climbing guide: it depends on the ratio of sound to decayed wood. If in doubt, avoid it.
