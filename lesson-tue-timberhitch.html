🔗 The Timber Hitch

A friction hitch for log handling and lashing starts. Grips under load, releases instantly when tension drops.

📋 Step-by-Step — How to Tie It

  1. Wrap the rope once around the object (log, spar, branch) in the direction you want to pull.
  2. Pass the working end under the standing part and back toward the object — forming a basic half-hitch over the standing part.
  3. Wrap the working end around itself 3 or more times, spiraling back toward the object. Each wrap should go in the direction that the pull will tighten — coil with the load, not against it.
  4. Dress and seat the knot: pull the standing part to cinch the coils snugly against the object. Under load, the hitch will self-tighten.
2

Minimum 3 wraps. Two wraps may slip under sustained or shock load — especially on smooth rope or wet wood. Three is the field standard; add a fourth on large-diameter or slippery material.

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

The timber hitch is the knot that arborists use when they're doing ground-based log management on steep Okanagan hillside removals — properties backing onto the escarpment above Lakeshore or on the Mission Road slopes. When you're dragging rounds to a landing zone or pulling a log away from a fence, the timber hitch lets you hook up fast and release instantly when you want to re-position. Practice it until it's automatic. At the end of a long removal day with a skid steer running and clients hovering, you don't want to be puzzling over your wraps.

⚙️ Load Characteristics & Limitations

What It Does Under Load

The timber hitch is a friction-dependent, self-tightening hitch. As tension increases on the standing part, the coils of the working end are drawn tighter against the object. This is its key strength — the harder you pull, the more securely it grips.

When tension is released, the coils loosen. The knot can then be untied quickly and easily — even after heavy loading — unlike a bowline or figure-8 on a bight that may need to be picked apart after shock loading.

When It Can Fail

  • Shifting load direction: The timber hitch is designed for a consistent pull along the axis of the object. If the load angle changes significantly, the hitch can walk or slip. Add a half-hitch toward the lead end to prevent walking.
  • Insufficient wraps: Two wraps is not enough on smooth or wet materials. Always use 3+.
  • Incompatible rope-to-object diameter ratio: Very large objects relative to rope diameter reduce friction area — increase wraps accordingly.

⚠ NOT a life-safety knot. The timber hitch is never used for climbing systems, lanyard attachment, or any application where a person's weight is suspended from the knot. Per ANSI Z133 and ISA BMP Rigging, life-safety connections require knots and hardware with documented working load limits and tested failure characteristics — the timber hitch provides neither.

Companion: Timber Hitch & Half-Hitch

For log dragging, add a single half-hitch a few feet up the log toward the direction of pull. This locks the lead end of the log, preventing it from swinging sideways and giving directional control. The timber hitch provides the main grip; the half-hitch steers. This combination is standard practice per ISA BMP Rigging for ground-based log handling operations.

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

ISA exam domain: Safe Work Practices. If you see a rigging question about which knot to use to attach a drag line to a log for skidding, the timber hitch is the correct answer. The question may also test whether you know what it can't do — and "life-safety suspension" is always the disqualifying application. Keep that boundary clear in your head. The knot is excellent at what it does. It just doesn't do everything.
