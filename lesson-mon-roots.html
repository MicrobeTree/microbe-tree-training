Tree Biology — Root Architecture & Mycorrhizal Partnerships

Monday Week 4 Microbe Tree Inc. · West Kelowna, BC

What's Underground Is Running the Show

Most tree problems — drought stress, decline, failed transplants, compaction damage — originate below the dripline, where nobody looks. A tree's root system can extend 2–3× the crown radius, yet 90% of absorptive roots live in the top 18–24 inches of soil. Understanding root architecture and mycorrhizal partnerships is the foundation of every PHC decision you'll make on site.

Five questions. Work through them in order. The lesson lives behind each answer — that's where the real material is.

BApproximately 70%, concentrated around the structural root flare
CApproximately 90%, because that zone has the highest oxygen, moisture, and nutrient availability
DApproximately 40%, with most roots following water tables to deeper horizons

Root Depth — Why Trees Are Shallow-Rooted

Roughly 90% of a tree's absorptive root biomass sits in the top 18–24 inches of soil. This surprises most people who picture deep taproots anchoring mature trees. The reality: taproots are a seedling structure. In most mature species, the taproot stops growing or becomes a structural anchor, and lateral roots dominate.

Why shallow? Three reasons:

  • Oxygen: Root respiration requires O₂. Soil gas exchange drops sharply below 12–24 inches, especially in compacted or saturated soils. Fine roots need aerated soil to function.
  • Moisture cycling: Rain, irrigation, and dew infiltrate from the top. Fine roots position themselves in the zone where water moves.
  • Nutrient stratification: Organic matter breakdown produces cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺) that accumulate in the A horizon. Roots follow nutrients.

The Critical Root Zone (CRZ) per ISA Best Management Practices is calculated at 1 foot of radius per inch of trunk DBH, with a minimum 5-foot radius. This is the no-damage perimeter for construction and grade changes — even shallow compaction or fill soil in this zone impairs gas exchange and kills fine roots.

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

In the Okanagan, our top 6 inches of soil often turn to concrete by late July — fractured silt loam with low organic content. When you drive a root zone and see compaction from equipment, the damage isn't just surface aesthetics. You've crushed gas exchange channels for roots that are trying to stay alive in the top 24 inches. Boulevard trees in West Kelowna suffer this constantly. Always flag the CRZ before any equipment enters the site — the tree's entire fine root network is right there under your feet.

DOrchid mycorrhizae — obligate for seed germination in Orchidaceae only

Ectomycorrhizae vs. Arbuscular Mycorrhizae — The Two You Need to Know

Ectomycorrhizae (ECM) are the dominant mycorrhizal type for most conifers and some hardwoods (oaks, birches, poplars). The fungal hyphae form a dense mantle (sheath) around the root tip, then grow between cortical cells to form the Hartig net — the interface where carbon and mineral exchange actually occurs. ECM do NOT penetrate root cells.

Arbuscular mycorrhizae (AM), formerly called vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizae (VAM), are the opposite — hyphae penetrate cortical cell walls and form branching arbuscules inside the cells. This is the most widespread type globally, colonizing roughly 80% of land plant species including most ornamental broadleaves, maples, fruit trees, and grasses.

Why it matters for PHC:

  • ECM fungi extend effective root surface area up to 1,000× — critical for phosphorus uptake in low-P Okanagan soils
  • ECM associations help trees access water in dry soils by extending hyphae into pore spaces roots cannot enter
  • Fertilizer applications (especially high N) can suppress mycorrhizal colonization — high nutrient availability reduces the tree's incentive to feed its fungal partners
  • Fumigants, fungicides, and soil disturbance destroy mycorrhizal networks — transplanted trees without active inoculants struggle
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

When we plant a new ponderosa pine or Douglas-fir on site, that tree is counting on finding ECM fungi in the soil. On disturbed urban soils — new subdivisions, post-construction sites — those fungal communities are gone. Applying a mycorrhizal inoculant at planting is not a premium upsell. It's basic PHC. Look for an inoculant that specifies ectomycorrhizal species (Pisolithus, Rhizopogon, Suillus) for conifers — the AM blends common at garden centres won't help your pines.

Roots Go Where the Crown Says They Don't

The most persistent myth in arboriculture: "roots stay under the canopy." They don't. Research cited in the ISA Certification Study Guide consistently shows mature trees extending lateral roots 2–3× the crown radius under good conditions. In open sandy or loam soils, some species extend even farther.

Root spread is driven by resource seeking, not anatomy. Roots follow:

  • Water: Irrigation lines, downspout discharge, creek margins, and lawn zones all attract root growth from far outside the canopy
  • Oxygen: Roots track aerobic soil corridors — often underneath sidewalks and pavement where traffic hasn't compacted the subgrade
  • Low-resistance pathways: Utility trenches, abandoned pipes, and construction fill channels become root highways

This is why construction damage 30 meters from a tree canopy still kills the tree. The roots are there — they just aren't visible from the surface. The CRZ (1 ft/inch DBH) is a minimum protection zone for worksites, not a description of actual root extent.

📋 Field Note — Okanagan

On client consultations where boulevard trees are failing near a driveway extension or new curb, the conversation often goes: "But we didn't touch the tree." Check whether any work happened within 10 meters of the trunk — trenching, grade change, compaction from equipment staging. In Kelowna's dry summer conditions, those trees are already running on thin margins. Cutting off roots 20 feet from the trunk in a drought year can be the last straw. Document what you see and connect the timeline for the client.

What Roots Actually Do — Four Core Functions

Photosynthesis requires chlorophyll and light — roots have neither. They are net consumers of the carbohydrates fixed by leaves, spending roughly 20–50% of photosynthate on root respiration, growth, and exudation. The four legitimate root functions are:

  • Absorption: Water and dissolved mineral ions enter through fine root tips and mycorrhizal hyphae. Root hairs (unicellular extensions of epidermal cells) dramatically increase surface area in non-mycorrhizal roots.
  • Anchorage: Structural roots — large, woody, lignified — anchor the tree against wind loads. Structural root failures are the primary cause of whole-tree windthrow. Root rot, girdling roots, and grade changes that kill structural roots all increase windthrow risk.
  • Storage: Roots store non-structural carbohydrates (NSC) — primarily starch — that fuel spring flush before the canopy is producing. A tree with depleted NSC reserves (from repeated defoliation, drought, or over-pruning) cannot flush properly and is at risk.
  • Synthesis: Roots produce cytokinins (growth-promoting hormones) and certain amino acids. Root health directly affects above-ground vigour. Drought-stressed roots produce less cytokinin → reduced shoot growth → visible decline.
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

The storage function is the one that bites us in August. A tree that defoliated in June from western spruce budworm, then baked in a July drought, has burned through most of its NSC reserves. When you're assessing it in late August and the crown looks "okay" — remember what it cost to push a second flush. That tree may look fine now and fail to survive winter or flush next spring. This is why PHC timing matters: biostimulant applications that support root health in spring, before stress events, are far more effective than trying to rescue a depleted tree in fall.

Q5 of 5

A large Norway maple on a client's property is showing dieback in the upper crown and thin foliage, despite adequate irrigation. The soil appears compacted. Which root-related diagnosis is most consistent with these symptoms?

AGirdling root — a circling root compressing the trunk and restricting vascular flow at the root collar
BRoot decay from Armillaria — rhizomorphs would be visible under bark at the root collar
CSoil compaction causing anaerobic conditions in the root zone, suppressing fine root function and mycorrhizal activity
DDrought-induced xylem embolism — the tree has cavitated and cannot move water despite surface moisture

Compaction — The Invisible Root Killer

All four answers describe real conditions, but the scenario — compacted soil + adequate irrigation + crown dieback — most classically points to soil compaction as the primary driver. Here's why:

Compaction increases soil bulk density, crushing macropores and eliminating the gas exchange that fine roots and mycorrhizal fungi depend on. Oxygen drops; CO₂ and anaerobic metabolites (ethanol, methane) accumulate. Fine roots die. Mycorrhizal colonization collapses. The tree loses its entire absorption surface even though water is present in the soil — it simply cannot uptake it or nutrients efficiently.

The paradox: Adequate irrigation doesn't fix compaction. Water sitting in compacted soil may actually worsen anaerobic conditions. The tree is effectively drowning while technically being "watered."

Field diagnosis progression:

  • Confirm compaction: penetrometer reading >300 psi in root zone = significant restriction
  • Excavate a small root zone sample — look for dark, mushy fine roots (anaerobic decay) vs. white healthy tips
  • Check for girdling root at root collar — always worth clearing any excess mulch and exposing the root flare
  • PHC prescription: vertical mulching, radial trenching filled with compost, air spade decompaction, and mycorrhizal inoculant
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Norway maples are everywhere on Kelowna boulevards, and compaction dieback is probably the most common thing we see on them. The diagnosis is almost never "needs more water." It's soil structure. When you open up the root zone with an air spade on one of these trees, you'll often find roots growing horizontally 2–3 inches below the surface because that's literally the only aerated zone. Everything below is a brick. Organic matter incorporation and decompaction are the treatment — not fertilizer, not irrigation adjustment. Fix the root environment first.
