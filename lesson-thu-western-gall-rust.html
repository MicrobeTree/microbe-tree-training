Western Gall Rust: Pine-to-Pine Galls and Hip Cankers

Round galls on lodgepole and ponderosa. Which ones are cosmetic, which ones break trees, and why now is the time to prune them.

🍄 The short answer

Western gall rust (Endocronartium harknessii, now often called Cronartium harknessii) is a fungus that makes round woody galls on the branches and stems of hard (2- and 3-needle) pines. Here that means lodgepole and ponderosa. Unlike most pine rusts, it goes pine to pine with no alternate host (NRCan). Branch galls are mostly cosmetic. Stem galls and hip cankers are a breakage risk.

Lodgepole, ponderosa; also Scots, Austrian, mugo
None: pine to pine
Orange, late May–early July
Usually 5–10 cm (NRCan)

🔎 What it looks like

  • Galls: NRCan says they're usually 5–10 cm across, and larger on the trunks of big trees if they persist. On ponderosa and lodgepole the gall is smooth and elongated, with "collars" where it pushes the bark back. Witches' brooms often follow.
  • In spring: galls break open into orange, powdery spore masses. Outside that window they're covered in bark (NRCan).
  • Old galls on dead branches are often stripped and grooved by rodent teeth (NRCan).
  • Hip canker: UC IPM says that when infection is at the base of the main stem, trees that live to an old age can develop a large depression on one side of the lower trunk.
  • Hosts: NRCan lists native hosts including lodgepole and ponderosa, plus planted exotics such as Scots, Austrian and mugo pine. That includes the Austrian pine from Week 21.

🔄 Life cycle

  • Spores infect the current year's shoots. The BC Southern Interior decision aid says galls start on 1-year-old growth and appear 1.5–2 years after infection. NRCan says spores appear 1–4 years after the gall starts.
  • Once a gall starts producing spores, it does so every year. Spores are released from late May to early July (NRCan). The decision aid says May–July, in warm, windy weather.
  • Infection varies hugely by year. Big infection years are called "wave years" (BC decision aid).
⚖️ Sources differ on spore travel. NRCan says spores generally spread within 100 m, and only rarely several kilometres in turbulent wind. The BC decision aid says they can travel hundreds of kilometres. The practical takeaway is the same: sanitation helps locally, but you can't fence it out.
⚠️ Breakage riskNRCan: hip and stem galls increase the chance of trunk breakage, and stem galls often kill the top or the whole tree. UC IPM: large galls on major limbs or the trunk can lead to structural failure. WSU: large galls can weaken the trunk or major limbs, making them highly susceptible to breakage. Don't tie in or rig above a stem gall or hip canker without assessing it, and treat it as a defect in any risk call.

🛠️ Management (no sprays)

  • Prune out branch galls. WSU says to prune out and destroy galls. UC IPM says to prune and dispose of infected branches October–January (California timing). Either way, it's before spring spore release, so now is the window.
  • Distance from the trunk matters. NRCan: branch galls are considered non-lethal if more than 15 cm from the trunk and pruned, or more than 60 cm away if not pruned. Galls closer than that can grow into the stem, so prioritize them.
  • Trunk galls: WSU says you may be able to cut back and train a new leader, but it may be necessary to remove the tree for safety. UC IPM says to consider removing and replacing hazardous trees.
  • Chemical: WSU lists none recommended.
  • In forests and plantations, BC's approach is stand-level: survey young stands, use spacing and density management, plant alternative species, and use resistant stock.
📋 Field Note: Okanagan

Ponderosa and lodgepole are both hosts here. On every pine you climb this winter, scan for round swellings: branch galls (prune them if close to the trunk), stem galls or a sunken hip on one side of the lower trunk (flag it for Anton, and plan your tie-in and rigging around it). Make sure the gall comes off with the branch, and get the prunings off site.

Question 1 of 5
What makes western gall rust different from most other pine stem rusts?
No alternate host
  • NRCan: it completes its highly reduced life cycle on pine without infecting an alternate host.
  • BC decision aid: infection occurs directly from pine to pine.
Question 2 of 5
Which trees are hosts in our area?
Hard pines
  • NRCan: found exclusively on hard (two- and three-needled) pines. Native hosts include lodgepole and ponderosa, and exotics include mugo, Austrian and Scots pine.
Question 3 of 5
What is a hip canker?
Basal stem infection
  • UC IPM: some pines develop infection at the base of the main stem. If they live to an old age, they can develop a large depression on one side of the lower trunk, called a hip canker.
  • NRCan: hip and stem galls increase the chance of trunk breakage.
Question 4 of 5
You find a branch gall on a ponderosa 10 cm from the trunk. What's the right move?
Close to the trunk = prune it now
  • NRCan: branch galls are non-lethal if located farther than 15 cm from the trunk and pruned, or 60 cm away and not pruned.
  • UC IPM: prune and dispose of infected branches October–January. WSU: prune out and destroy galls.
