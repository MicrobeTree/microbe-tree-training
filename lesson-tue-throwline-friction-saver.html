🎯 Why this is a Safe Work topic

Your tie-in is your life-support anchor, and it's set from the ground with a 300-gram bag. Safe Work Practices is 15% of the ISA Certified Arborist exam, the biggest single domain. Its tasks include determining whether the tree can withstand the forces of the work, inspecting tools and equipment before use, and selecting climbing equipment that meets regulations and industry standards.

Question 1 of 5
You're setting a throwline on a 15 m Douglas-fir union. Where do you aim, and what do you do after a miss?
Repeatable throws, clean line

From TCI Magazine's "University of Throw Line":

  • Aim about a foot above the branch. Aim right at it and you'll hit the branch, not the airspace over it.
  • Pick one throwing method (one-handed side throw or two-handed underhand/cradle throw) and repeat it exactly. Keep your body still so the bag swings in a straight arc, and your feet in the same spot.
  • After a miss, flake the line back into the cube. Line thrown off the ground snags on bark, sticks, weeds and your boots.
  • Keep the cube free of leaves and debris.
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

Dry, dusty fall canopies and pine-needle litter get into everything. Throw from a cube or a clean tarp, never off the dirt, and shake the grit out of your line at the end of the day.

Question 2 of 5
Your bag sailed over a union higher than the one you wanted. What's the cleanest fix?
Isolate, don't yank
  • Isolating: work the weight from the ground so it falls on your side of the lower branch and down to the ground. Tie a second weight to the other end and pull it up and over to the far side of the target branch. That leaves the line over only the branch you want.
  • Double-bagging: two cubes, two weights joined with a double-locking carabiner so the gate can't open in the tree. Use two different colours so you always know which line is over the limb.
  • Why not yank? If a single weight gets stuck and you yank it, the branch may break, or the weight pops out "with great force" and flies uncontrolled a long way (TCI).
⚠ Crew ruleHelmet and eye protection on while throwing or freeing a stuck bag. Nobody stands in the drop zone or in line with a line under tension.
Question 3 of 5
What does a friction saver (cambium saver) actually do for the tree and for you?
Protect the bark, protect the rope
  • Branch protection (savers, sleeves) protects the tree from damage by the climber's rope. On a moving-rope system, rope sawing over a union scrapes bark and can wound the cambium underneath. Sleeves and savers also reduce friction on the rope, which extends rope life and makes climbing easier (Tree Climbers International).
  • CMI's instructions add: less bend radius and less rope wear because the rope no longer touches bark, consistent friction as you move, and it can be wrapped on a stem where no natural crotch exists.
  • Ring-and-ring: the rope runs through both rings. Pull the rope through the wrong end and it jams in the tree (TCI).
  • Retrieval (CMI): a retrieval line with a retrieval ball goes on the climbing line and through the large ring, and the saver is lowered to the ground.
⚠ Manufacturer limitsInstall only on structure that can take the loads, with no sharp edges against the webbing or rings. Never climb above the anchor point, and keep slack out of the system (CMI).
Question 4 of 5
Your throwline went over the target union, but on the way down it drapes over two other limbs. Why should you clean that up before tying in?
Choose your redirects. Don't inherit them.
  • ISA's Certified Tree Climber exam outline expects you to redirect rope around branches for an uninterrupted path to the ground, and to assess branch strength (attachment angle, diameter, decay) for anything that will carry load.
  • A redirect is a load point. On a moving-rope system (MRS), redirect limbs take dynamic strain. TCI notes that SRS lets you redirect onto small limbs that "would never absorb the dynamic strain" of an MRS redirect. Either way, it's a limb you have to choose and judge.
  • An accidental drape also rubs bark where there's no saver, adds friction to every move, and can snag your descent.

Fix it on the ground: work the throwline clear (isolate again, or pull the line back through and re-set) before the climbing line goes up. Then proof-load test the anchor before committing (ISA Certified Tree Climber outline).

Question 5 of 5
What's the right inspection routine for a ring-and-ring friction saver?
Pre-use, every time, plus a second set of eyes
  • CMI friction saver instructions: before each use check for cuts, burning on the web from abrasion, cut or loose load-bearing stitching and fading or degradation from wear or UV. If any are present, retire and destroy it. Also check rings for wear and make sure the parts sit correctly relative to each other. A hard fall or misuse can shorten its life sharply.
  • TCI (Tresselt, "Equipment and Gear Inspection"): inspect all life-support gear immediately before use, and do deeper, less frequent inspections on top of that. Document them with a simple checklist. Have someone else inspect your gear periodically to beat "my-gear blindness."
  • Inspecting PPE and climbing equipment is listed in ISA's Safe Work Practices domain.
