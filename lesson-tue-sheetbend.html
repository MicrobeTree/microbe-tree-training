Sheet Bend

The knot for joining two lines that don't match. Simple geometry, important limitations.

📐 The Knot

🔢 Tying Sequence

1
Form a bight in the thicker or stiffer rope — make a J-shape with the working end alongside the standing part.
2
Pass the thinner rope up through the bight from below — working end comes up through the J opening.
3
Route the thinner rope around the back of both legs of the bight — go behind the thicker rope entirely.
4
Tuck the working end under itself only — not under the bight strands. Tuck on the same side as the bight tail. Dress and set.
⚠ Critical check: Both free ends (the thicker rope tail and the thinner rope working end) must exit on the SAME SIDE of the knot. If they exit on opposite sides, the knot is incorrectly tied and will capsize under load. This is the #1 error with the sheet bend.

Double Sheet Bend: For significant diameter differences, take an extra wrap with the thinner rope before tucking under itself. Same rules apply — more holding power, same geometry.

✅ When to Use It

  • Joining two ropes of noticeably different diameters
  • Extending a throwline with different-diameter line
  • Attaching a tagline to a heavier rigging line (non-critical)
  • Field repairs where mismatched cordage is all you have
  • Attaching rope to a fixed loop or sling eye (bight version)

🚫 When NOT to Use It

  • Life-safety rigging — not rated for critical loads
  • Worker or patient attachment at any point
  • Joining ropes of the same diameter (bowline-to-bowline or double fisherman's is stronger)
  • Any application subject to intermittent loading or shock loads
  • Wet rope with significant diameter difference (can slip)

⚖️ Load Characteristics & Standards

Property Sheet Bend Double Sheet Bend
Strength retention (approx.) ~45–60% of weaker rope ~55–65% of weaker rope
Slippage risk under cyclic load Moderate — monitor under load Lower — extra wrap adds security
Ease of untying after heavy load Usually easy Slightly harder
ANSI Z133 critical-load rating Not rated — ANSI Z133 does not endorse the sheet bend for life-safety or critical rigging loads
WorkSafeBC classification Not listed as an approved life-safety connection method in OHS Regulation — use rated hardware (carabiners, shackles) for critical connections
✓ Per ISA BMP Rigging: Knot selection should match the application and expected load. For non-critical line connections during rigging operations (e.g. taglines, guiding lines), the sheet bend is acceptable. For any load-bearing connection in a critical rigging system, use appropriately rated hardware with documented WLL.

🔄 Know the Difference

Knot Best for Limitation
Sheet Bend Joining two ropes of different diameters Not life-rated; can slip if ropes vary greatly in stiffness
Bowline-to-Bowline Joining two ropes of similar diameter where loops are formed Bulky; ropes must be fairly equal
Double Fisherman's Joining two ropes of similar material and diameter (Prusik loops, rescue lines) Very difficult to untie after loading; ropes must match well
Alpine Butterfly Creating a loop in the middle of a rope; multi-directional loading Not for joining two separate ropes
📋 Field Note (Okanagan): You'll reach for a sheet bend most often when extending a throwline mid-job — your 1.75mm throw line runs out and all you have left is a heavier 3mm polypropylene line. The sheet bend joins them fast and gets the weighted bag into the canopy. That's exactly what this knot is for. Just remember: once you've made the connection and pulled the rigging rope through, untie and re-rig with proper matched cordage or a rated connector before you put any meaningful load on it.
