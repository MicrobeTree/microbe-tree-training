Orchard (Tripod) Ladder Footing & Rubber Mats

A tripod ladder is only as safe as its three feet. What WorkSafeBC Part 13 requires, how Microbe sets up on slick or soft ground, and the inspection that keeps it that way.

Learning From Our Own Near-Miss

We recently had a ladder incident on our crew. We're not sharing who was involved or any personal details. Nobody needs those to learn from it, and they stay private. What we can all use is the lesson: a tripod ladder is only as good as its footing, and footing is the step most likely to get rushed at the end of a long day.

This lesson covers what WorkSafeBC requires, how Microbe sets up on slippery or unstable ground, and how to inspect the ladder and the mats.

📋 Field Note — Microbe Tree Crew

If you saw something on that job, or you have an idea that would have prevented it, tell Anton directly. Near-miss reports aren't about blame. They're how the next one doesn't happen.

📐 OSHA's tripod ladder fact sheet: falls are the most common ladder hazard, more falls happen on the upper part of the ladder, and most are due to slippery steps, unstable ladders and not keeping proper contact with the ladder.

WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation, Part 13: What Applies to Us

  • §13.2 Standards: a ladder must meet, and be used according to, the applicable CSA or ANSI standard in effect when it was made (or another standard the Board accepts).
  • §13.3 Inspections: ladders must be inspected before use on each shift and after any modification. Anything that could endanger a worker must be fixed before the ladder is used.
  • §13.4 Manufactured ladders: a manufactured portable ladder must be marked with its grade of material and its intended use. No legible label means no proof it's the right ladder.
  • §13.5(1) Position and stability: a portable ladder must be placed on a firm and level surface and be long enough to do the work safely.
  • §13.5(2) (the ~75° angle, 1 m above the landing, tie-off rules) is written for non-self-supporting ladders. A tripod orchard ladder is self-supporting, so §13.5(1) is our footing rule.
  • §13.6 Use restrictions: if the work can't be done from a ladder without hazard, a work platform must be provided. Don't carry heavy, bulky or awkward objects up or down a ladder.

What about Part 28 (Agriculture)?

Part 28 defines an orchard ladder (§28.1: a self-supporting tripod ladder, wide at the bottom, non-adjustable, with a hinged single-leg back) and sets orchard-ladder design rules (§28.26). These include steel points or other non-slip bases designed for the ground, when needed for safety. It also exempts orchard ladders from some Part 13 rules (§28.25). But §28.2 says Part 28 applies to agricultural operations on farm land. Our residential, strata and municipal jobs aren't that, so Part 13 applies in full. If a job is on a working farm or orchard, check with Anton first.

Footing on Slippery or Unstable Ground

How a tripod ladder is meant to stand

  • OSHA: tripod orchard ladders have a flared base and a tripod pole for soft, uneven ground and no spreader bar or locks. To stay stable, the side rails and tripod pole must slightly penetrate the ground.
  • Set it firmly in the ground, and not over a soft spot or hole.
  • On a slope, the tripod pole goes uphill.
  • OSHA also says: never use these ladders on hard surfaces.
🟩 Microbe standard: on slippery or unstable ground (wet leaves, frost or dew on turf, loose bark mulch, muddy or soft soil), we put rubber mats under the ladder footings.

What the mat is for, and what it isn't

  • For: giving a foot something to grip instead of skating on a slick surface, and spreading the load so one leg doesn't punch into a soft spot.
  • Not for: making a hard surface OK. Pavement, decks, patio stone and ice stay no-go areas for a tripod ladder, mat or no mat (OSHA: never on hard surfaces).
  • The mat must sit flat and stay put. A mat that slides on wet grass or a slope just moves the slip one layer down.
  • The test: after setup, stand on the bottom step and shift your weight side to side and front to back. Any rocking, sliding or sinking means reset. If it still won't sit firm and level (§13.5(1)(a)), it isn't a ladder spot. Use another access method or a work platform (§13.6(1)).

Setup Checklist (Every Move)

  1. Inspect first: pre-shift check done and label legible (§13.3, §13.4).
  2. Right ladder: long enough to reach without the top three steps (§13.5(1)(b); OSHA).
  3. Read the ground: soft, slick, sloped, hollow or hard? Hard surface → not a tripod ladder spot.
  4. Clear and mat: rake off wet leaves and debris. On slippery or unstable ground, lay the rubber mats flat under the footings (Microbe standard).
  5. Seat it: rails and pole seated firmly, pole uphill on a slope, not over a hole or soft spot (OSHA).
  6. Weight test on the bottom step. Reset until there's no rock, slide or sink (§13.5(1)(a)).
  7. Overhead check: mark and avoid electrical lines (OSHA).
  8. Climb right: one worker per ladder, centred between the rails, both hands on the way up and down, no overreaching (move the ladder instead), and never step off onto a branch or use one for balance (OSHA).
  9. Tools: nothing heavy or bulky carried up or down (§13.6(2)). Hand it up or use a tool line.
  10. Weather: limit or reschedule ladder work in high wind, heavy rain or thunderstorms (OSHA).
  11. End of day: never leave a ladder standing open (OSHA).
📋 Field Note — Okanagan

October in the valley means wet leaf mats, heavy morning dew and the first frost on shaded turf. The lawn that was fine at 2 pm can be slick at 8 am. Reassess the footing every time you move the ladder, not just at the first setup.

Inspect the Ladder, and the Mats

Ladder (before each shift, after any change: §13.3)

  • Rivets: missing, loose or sheared (OSHA).
  • Steps: broken, cracked or loose (OSHA).
  • Rails and tripod pole: bent (OSHA). Check the pole hinge and the feet.
  • Label present and legible (§13.4).
  • After any tip-over, inspect it top to bottom before it goes back in service (OSHA).
⚠ Damaged = out of serviceTag it "out of service" and keep it apart from good ladders. Workers don't repair ladders (OSHA). §13.3 requires any hazard to be fixed before the ladder is used again.

Rubber mats (Microbe standard)

  • Torn, split, curled or worn smooth → replace.
  • Oily, muddy or icy → clean and dry before use. A slick mat is worse than no mat.
  • Store them flat and dry with the ladders so they're on the truck when you need them.

Storage

Store ladders per the manufacturer's instructions, away from excessive heat and cold (OSHA).

Tailgate Quick Check

  1. Which Part 13 section requires a firm and level surface? (§13.5(1)(a))
  2. When must the ladder be inspected? (Before use on each shift, and after any modification: §13.3)
  3. Where does the tripod pole go on a slope? (Uphill)
  4. What's the mat for, and where is a tripod ladder a no-go even with a mat? (Grip and load spread on slick or soft ground. Never on hard surfaces.)
  5. The ladder still rocks after two resets. What now? (Not a ladder spot. Use another access method or a work platform: §13.6(1).)
